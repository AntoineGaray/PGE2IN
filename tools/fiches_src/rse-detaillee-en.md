## Session 1 – Today's major issues

Environmental and social issues are at the heart of the survival and robustness of companies. The Regenerative Company Fresk is used to identify ecological and human dependencies and impacts along the **value chain**.

### The regenerative economy (definition)

**Regeneration** means going beyond reducing negative impacts or neutralising them, to commit to generating **net positive impacts** for ecosystems and society.

### The findings

- **Fossil fuels**, since the Industrial Revolution (~1850), are "our magic wand to transform the world". Their consumption continues to increase despite the arrival of renewable energies.
- **The time scale**: the Earth is 4.6 billion years old, life 4 billion, *Homo sapiens* 100,000 years, agriculture and sedentarization 12,000 years.
- **Climate**: the latest IPCC report dates from 2023. Examples of extreme events: Valencia (autumn 2024), California (January 2025), Mayotte (winter 2024).
- **The financial statement of summer 2026 in France**:
    - Drought: more than **€5.6 billion** of insurance costs, mainly the shrinkage-swelling of clays (cracked houses). This is above the historical record of 2022.
    - Agricultural losses: more than **€1 billion**.
    - Productivity: above 30°C, hourly efficiency drops drastically (Allianz Trade study).
    - The Saumos megafire (Gironde): **42,000 hectares**, 240 buildings destroyed, **220,000 people evacuated**.
    - Health: four successive heat waves, nearly **€5 billion** for the Social Security system.
    - Total insured damage and direct losses: **€10 to 12 billion**.
- **Inequalities**: wealth inequalities go hand in hand with CO2 emission inequalities (source: Oxfam).
- **Social**: the collapse of **Rana Plaza** (Bangladesh, April 2013), a textile factory.
- **Biodiversity**:
    - **25%** of living species threatened with extinction.
    - **35%** of pollinator species used for human consumption have disappeared in France.
    - **75%** of the Earth's surface is significantly altered.
    - The rate of species extinction is **100 to 1,000 times** higher than the natural rate: we speak of the **6th mass extinction**.
- **The 5 causes of biodiversity decline** (IPBES): 1) land and sea use changes, 2) direct exploitation of certain species, 3) climate change, 4) soil, water and air pollution, 5) spread of invasive alien species.
- **Overshoot Day**: humanity uses more than one Earth a year. **The Great Acceleration** refers to the surge of all indicators since 1950.
- Moving **from the triangle of inaction to the triangle of action**: companies, citizens and the State each wait for the others to move. The aim is to break out of that waiting game.

## Session 2 – The donut

Kate Raworth's donut combines an **environmental ceiling** (9 planetary boundaries) and a **social floor** (12 needs). Between the two lies the safe and just space in which humanity can thrive.

### The limits to growth: the Meadows Report (1972)

- Commissioned in 1970 by the **Club of Rome** from researchers at **MIT**. Published in 1972 as *The Limits to Growth*, it is named after its lead authors Donella and Dennis Meadows. It is based on the **World3** computer model (population, industrial growth, food production, limits of terrestrial ecosystems). It came out before the first oil crisis of 1973.
- The findings: world population went from 500 million (17th century, +0.3% a year, doubling every 250 years) to 3.6 billion (early 1970s, doubling every 32 years). Industrial production grew by about 7% a year in the 1960s, i.e. doubling every 10 years.
- The risks: food resources and drinking water (diminishing returns), pollution (which also grows exponentially), energy resources.
- The conclusion: if nothing changes, the limits will be reached within the next hundred years, with a **sudden and uncontrollable decline**. Sustainable ecological and economic stability is still possible, and the sooner we start, the greater the chances of success.
- Line to remember: "infinite growth in a finite world is not sustainable".

### The 9 planetary boundaries

Proposed by **Johan Rockström** and a group of 28 scientists in **2009** (Stockholm Resilience Centre). Planetary boundaries are the safe limits for human pressure on the nine critical processes that maintain a stable and resilient Earth. The context: for 10,000 years, stable climatic conditions have allowed civilisations to flourish.

1. Novel entities (plastics, chemicals)
2. Stratospheric ozone depletion
3. Atmospheric aerosol loading
4. Ocean acidification
5. Biogeochemical flows (nitrogen and phosphorus cycles)
6. Freshwater change
7. Land-system change
8. Biosphere integrity (biodiversity)
9. Climate change

**Seven of the nine boundaries are violated.** Crossing them increases the risk of abrupt or irreversible large-scale changes (**tipping points**). The boundaries are **interdependent**: crossing one affects the others.

### The climate change example

| Indicator | Value |
| --- | --- |
| CO2 in 1850 | 280 ppm |
| Limit recommended by scientists | 350 ppm |
| CO2 in 2025 | **426 ppm** |
| Critical threshold (irreversible +2°C) | 450 ppm |
| Warming already observed | **+1.1°C** compared with 1850-1900 |
| Paris Agreement goal | Well below 2°C, if possible 1.5°C |

**Radiative forcing** is the difference between the energy entering the atmosphere and the energy escaping into space. If it is greater than zero, the planet is warming.

Negative impacts for a business:

- Supply chain disruptions.
- Rising insurance and risk management costs.
- Physical damage to infrastructure.
- Regulatory compliance costs (carbon taxes).
- Reputational risk.
- Water or energy stress.
- Volatility in raw material and energy prices.
- Difficulties accessing financing (ESG criteria).
- Impact on employee health and productivity.
- Migration of talent or customers away from climate-risk areas.

Positive impacts:

- New markets related to low-carbon solutions.
- Competitive advantage for pioneers in sustainability.
- Increased appeal to ESG investors.
- Innovation driven by the need to adapt.
- Long-term cost reductions through energy efficiency.
- Retention of talent.
- Government grants and incentives.
- Brand differentiation and customer loyalty.
- Anticipating regulatory changes.
- New business models, such as the circular economy.

### Biodiversity and pollution (slides not covered in class, but in the material)

- **Ecosystem services**, in 4 families:
    - **Procurement (provisioning) services**: food, freshwater, wood and fuels, fibre, medicinal resources.
    - **Regulatory services**: air quality and climate control, soil quality, flood control, crop pollination.
    - **Cultural services**: tourism, heritage, inspiration (biomimicry).
    - **Support services**: photosynthesis, soil formation, nutrient cycling.
- The quiz question on the share of world GDP that depends on nature (10, 25, 33 or 55%): the expected answer is **55%**. The PDF does not give it; it is the well-known IPBES and WEF figure (more than half of GDP).
- Other figures: 1/3 of land area is used for agriculture, natural forest area has fallen by **42% since 1700**, 33% of fish stocks are overexploited, invasive alien species have increased by 76% in 35 years (example: the coypu), and we eat about **5 g of plastic per week** (a credit card).
- Air pollution: more than 8 million deaths in 2018 according to Harvard, and 417,000 premature deaths in Europe in 2020 according to the European Environment Agency.

### The Doughnut Theory (Kate Raworth, Oxfam, 2017)

1. **Environmental ceiling** = the 9 planetary boundaries.
2. **Social floor** = **12 social floors**, below which lie many dimensions of human deprivation.
3. **Between the two**: an **ecologically safe and socially just space** in which humanity can thrive, the space of inclusive and sustainable economic development.

| Social floor | Course figure |
| --- | --- |
| Food | 800 million people undernourished |
| Health | Less than half the world's population has access to essential healthcare |
| Education | More than 250 million children not in school |
| Income & work | More than 2 billion people in poverty, half a billion underemployed |
| Water | Billions without safe drinking water or sanitation |
| Energy | Nearly 800 million without electricity, 3 billion cooking with wood, dung or kerosene |
| Networks | Half without local public transport, over 40% without internet at home, 1 in 4 with no one to turn to |
| Housing | Nearly 1 billion people living in slums |
| Gender equality | Measured by the gender representation gap in parliaments and the gender pay gap |
| Social equity | Measured by the Palma index (income share of the richest 10% over the poorest 40%), threshold of 2 |
| Political voice | Half the world's population lives without sufficient guarantees |
| Peace & justice | More than 70 million people forcibly displaced |

The donut opens three perspectives: 1) **an integrated vision** of the social, environmental and economic dimensions, 2) **a refocusing of economic priorities**: social and environmental issues are no longer portrayed as economic "externalities", 3) **metrics beyond GDP** (tonnes of carbon emitted, number of people facing hunger).

### The donut individual work method (reusable for an exam case)

1. Present the company and its value chain, from its supplies to the end of life of its products.
2. List its positive and negative impacts on the 9 planetary boundaries.
3. List its impacts on the 12 social floors.
4. Deduce the risks and opportunities for the company.

**The L'Oréal example** (founded in 1909 by chemist Eugène Schueller, present in more than 150 countries):

- Negative impacts on biodiversity: land use and agricultural pressure from plant-based raw materials, formula residues and microplastics released when rinsing, indirect carbon footprint, deforestation risks.
- Positive impacts: sustainable sourcing of ingredients, eco-designed formulas and packaging (recycled plastic, FSC-certified cardboard), ecosystem restoration, the **SPOT** measurement tool.
- Risks follow a typology worth learning: **physical risk** (raw material shortages), **transition risk** (tighter regulations on deforestation), **reputational risk** ("greenwashing").
- Opportunities: innovation through "Green Sciences", value chain security and resilience, preferential access to ESG capital.

## Session 3 – History, definitions, standards and tools

CSR went from an idea (1953) to a legal obligation (CSRD, 2022) in four phases. The dates are very likely MCQ questions.

### The CSR timeline

| Date | Event | Key point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1953** | **Bowen**, *Social Responsibilities of the Businessman* | He created the term "social responsibility" |
| **1962** | **Rachel Carson**, *Silent Spring* | The dangers of pesticides, which she argues should be renamed "biocides". The birth of modern environmental awareness |
| **1972** | **Stockholm** UN Conference on the Human Environment | Creation of the **United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP)**. Also the year of the Meadows Report |
| 1973 and 1979 | Oil shocks | Oil prices quadruple (OPEC embargo), then the Iranian revolution. Inflation and recession **slow corporate responsibility** |
| **1976** | **OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises** | Human rights, environment, anti-corruption, taxation, disclosure of information |
| **1984** | **Freeman**, stakeholder theory | Businesses are a system that creates value for all their stakeholders |
| **1987** | **The Brundtland Report** | First definition of sustainable development (see below) |
| **1992** | **Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro** | More than 178 countries adopt **Agenda 21**. 27 principles in 4 themes: solidarity, precaution, responsibility, participation. The UNFCCC is adopted |
| **1997** | **Kyoto Protocol** (COP 3) | Binding on industrialized countries: GHG emissions at least 5% below 1990 levels (5.2% on average) over 2008-2012. 6 gases: CO2, CH4, N2O, HFC, PFC, SF6 |
| **1999** | **UN Global Compact** | Proposed by Kofi Annan in Davos |
| **2000** | Millennium Declaration | The **8 Millennium Development Goals (MDGs)** to reduce extreme poverty by 2015 |
| **2001** | European Commission **Green Paper** on CSR | It formalized CSR as a concept |
| 2002 | Johannesburg Summit | Reaffirms Agenda 21 and the Millennium Declaration |
| **2010** | **ISO 26000** | A universal international standard, **not certifiable** |
| **2014** | **Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD)** (2014/95/EU) | A mandatory "non-financial statement" for large public-interest entities with more than 500 employees |
| 2012 | **Rio+20**, "The Future We Want" | Launches the SDG process (the slide says 2021 by mistake) |
| **2015** | **2030 Agenda** and the **17 SDGs** | — |
| **2015** | **Paris Agreement** (COP 21) | Well below 2°C, if possible 1.5°C. Over 2012-2021, the Earth was already +1.2°C (IPCC) |
| **2019-2020** | **European Green Deal** (Ursula von der Leyen) | Climate neutrality by 2050, GHG −55% by 2030 compared with 1990, around 50 actions in 8 key areas |
| **2022** | **Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)** | Improves on the NFRD. Targets all large companies, listed or not, listed SMEs and some non-EU companies (over €150 million of turnover in the EU). Implementation is delayed |

The course's four phases: 1) the beginnings of sustainable development, 2) the rise of CSR in the economic world, 3) institutionalization and regulation, 4) towards strategic and integrated CSR.

### Definitions

- **Sustainable development (Brundtland, 1987)**: "meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs".
- **ISO 26000 (2010)**: the responsibility of an organization for the impacts of its decisions and activities on society and the environment, resulting in ethical and transparent behaviour that:
    - contributes to sustainable development, including the health and well-being of society;
    - takes into account the expectations of stakeholders;
    - complies with applicable laws and is consistent with international standards of behaviour;
    - is integrated throughout the organization and implemented in its relationships.
- **European Commission (2011)**: the **voluntary** integration of social and environmental concerns by companies into their business activities and their relations with their stakeholders. Another wording: "the responsibility of companies with regard to the effects they have on society". The CSRD adds three requirements: identify, prevent and mitigate negative effects; report on social, environmental, ethical and governance impacts; go beyond regulatory compliance.
- **Global Compact (UN)**: aligning a company's strategies and operations with **ten universal principles** in 4 areas: human rights, labour, the environment and the fight against corruption.

### The example covered in class: the SDGs

- **Nature**: adopted in September 2015 by the **193 UN member states** in the 2030 Agenda. **17 goals, 169 targets, 244 indicators**. A **purely voluntary** framework: no legal enforcement, no sanctions, free choice of priorities.
- **Target audience**: universal ("Leave no one behind"). For large multinationals, a tool for structuring non-financial reporting. For SMEs, a simple reading grid. For start-ups, a basis for an impact model. It also serves associations and the public sector. The economic potential is estimated at more than €10,000 billion by some studies.
- **How it works**:
    1. **Mapping** the 17 SDGs against the value chain and selecting 3 to 5 priority SDGs according to materiality.
    2. **Strategic integration**: validation by management or the board, choice of KPIs (example: measuring the gender pay gap for SDG 5, Gender Equality).
    3. **Valorisation and management**: reporting, free tools such as the **SDG Compass**, no external audit required.
- **Strengths**: a common language, a stronger employer brand, innovation.
- **Limits**: **SDG washing** (logos used as marketing "stickers"), opportunistic selection (displaying SDG 8 Economic Growth and ignoring SDG 12 Responsible Consumption), and targets designed for governments, not for corporate accounting.
- **Example**: Carrefour concentrates on 8 SDGs (2025 Universal Registration Document).

| Tool | Nature | Who is concerned | How it works | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

| **Eco-design and LCA** | Voluntary approach, ISO 14040 and 14044 standards | Companies that make products | **Life Cycle Assessment** measures **multi-criteria** impacts "from cradle to grave" (materials, manufacturing, transport, use, end of life) in order to eco-design | Heavy, highly dependent on data and assumptions |

## Session 4 – ISO 26000 and the CSR strategic plan

ISO 26000 is the "universal dictionary of CSR". It is **voluntary and non-certifiable** and rests on **7 central questions**. It is used to diagnose a company, then to build its action plan. It is the likely core of the case studies.

### The three pillars of CSR

The objective is to create a balance between the economic, environmental and social dimensions, at the intersection of the three circles of sustainable development.

| Pillar | Key points | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Economic responsibility** | Profitability, fair business practices (transparency, anti-corruption, fair competition), innovation and investment, supply chain management | Transparent financial reporting, job creation and support for local businesses, fair prices |
| **Social responsibility** | Employee well-being, diversity and inclusion, community involvement, human rights | Training, work-life balance, charitable donations and volunteering, preventing discrimination |
| **Environmental responsibility** | Sustainable use of resources, pollution prevention, climate action, biodiversity conservation | Environmentally friendly materials, renewable energy, waste recycling, less packaging |

### The ISO 26000 standard

- Published in **2010** by the ISO, after **5 years of negotiations** between **99 countries** and hundreds of experts (companies, governments, NGOs, trade unions).
- **Voluntary and non-certifiable**: it contains guidelines, not requirements. Unlike ISO 9001 (quality) or ISO 14001 (environment), a company cannot be "ISO 26000 certified". It can only be "evaluated" or "aligned with" the standard.
- It concerns **all organisations**: large multinationals, SMEs, start-ups, public administrations, local authorities, universities, associations.
- Its approach is **holistic**: economic, social (internal and external) and environmental.

### The 7 central questions

| # | Central question | Example issues | What to look at | Where to find the information |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Organizational governance** | Gender diversity on the management committee, financial transparency | A CSR Director on the Comex, or under HR and communication? % of women on the board? Variable executive pay indexed to CSR goals? Taxes paid where profits are made? | Annual report, Universal Registration Document, organisational chart |
| 2 | **Human rights** | Equal pay, child labour at subcontractors | A vigilance plan? Mapping of Tier 1, 2 and 3 suppliers? Anonymous CVs? Freedom to join unions in foreign subsidiaries? | NGO reports (Amnesty, Human Rights Watch), ethical charter, investigative press |
| 3 | **Relations and working conditions** | Social dialogue, occupational safety, work/life balance | Frequency and severity rate of workplace accidents, burnout, agreements signed with the CSE, turnover, absenteeism, profit-sharing and employee shareholding | Social report, Glassdoor (with critical distance), CSR report |
| 4 | **The environment** | Pollution prevention, climate change mitigation, biodiversity | A carbon footprint including Scope 3? % of waste recycled? Share of renewable energy? A water sobriety plan? Deforestation (palm oil, soy)? | ADEME BEGES platform, sustainability report, ISO 14001 certifications |
| 5 | **Fair practices** | Anti-corruption, fair competition | An anonymous whistleblowing system? CSR criteria in purchasing (e.g. 20% of the score)? EcoVadis? Payment terms? Lobbying? | Code of business conduct, HATVP, Competition Authority sanctions |
| 6 | **Consumer issues** | Transparency, product safety, data protection | Product recalls? Greenwashing (Advertising Ethics Jury)? Data leaks (GDPR)? Accessible or solidarity offers? | RappelConso, customer reviews, GTCU, JDP decisions |
| 7 | **Communities and local development** | Local jobs, partnerships with schools | Local hiring, work-study students from priority neighbourhoods, skills-based sponsorship | Local press, press releases, corporate foundation reports |

### How the company deals with it in practice

- **Self-diagnosis**: mapping of stakeholders and identification of priority issues (**materiality**).
- **External evaluation**: since the standard is not certifiable, companies use CSR labels such as **AFNOR "Engagé RSE"** or **Lucie 26000**. They give a maturity score: Initial, Progression, Confirmed, Exemplary.
- **Strengths**: a unified global framework, a global approach that is not limited to "the green", an ideal structuring tool for a first CSR roadmap.
- **Limitations**: no binding control (risk of green/fairwashing), a heavy text (more than 100 pages), costly derived labels for small SMEs.

### The diagnosis method (to apply in an exam case)

For each of the 7 pillars, answer three questions with concrete data (CSR report, press, website, testimonials):

1. **What is being done?** Actions and related KPIs.
2. **What are the weaknesses?**
3. **What is the evidence?**

Present the result in a **CSR maturity table**: a level for each question (**Insufficient** = major risk, **Intermediate** = can do better, **Advanced** = best practices), a 2 to 3-sentence summary and the list of evidence.

The course's mock example, a fashion brand:

- Governance **Intermediate**: CSR reports to Communication, but 15% of managers' variable pay is indexed to waste reduction, and the board has 45% women.
- Human rights **Insufficient**: 60% of subcontracting factories are in risk countries, and a Tier 2 supplier uses forced labour.
- Working conditions **Advanced**: 3 days of remote work, a gender equality index of 92/100, turnover of 6% versus 18% in the sector.
- Environment **Intermediate**: Scopes 1 and 2 are falling, but Scope 3 is exploding with 80% virgin polyester.
- Fair practices **Intermediate**: payment terms of 65 days, beyond the legal limit.
- Consumers **Insufficient**: the "100% Green Collection" campaign was sanctioned when only 5% of the products used organic cotton.
- Communities **Advanced**: an integration programme and 2 paid volunteering days per employee.

### Building the CSR strategic plan: the environment, based on the value chain

The logic to follow is the **ERC sequence + R**: **Avoid** any unnecessary impact, then **Reduce** (sobriety, eco-design, efficiency), then **Offset** as a last resort, and finally **Regenerate** (leave nature in a better state than before).

| Link in the chain | Levers |
| --- | --- |
| I. Raw material procurement and purchasing (upstream) | Zero deforestation, no pollinator-killing pesticides, regenerative agriculture, material sobriety, recycled, upcycled or biosourced materials |
| II. R&D & product design | **80% of a product's environmental impact is determined during its design phase**. Multi-criteria LCA (to avoid pollution transfers), design for disassembly, fight against obsolescence, *Cradle to Cradle* |
| III. Internal operations & manufacturing | Energy decarbonization (Scopes 1 and 2: efficiency, heat pumps, photovoltaics). Water: sobriety, **Zero Liquid Discharge**, water returned cleaner than when withdrawn. Zero Waste to Landfill, **industrial symbiosis** |
| IV. Logistics & transport | Regionalization, fill rate, **modal shift** (rail, inland waterways), electrify the fleet and the last mile |
| V. Product use & end of life (downstream) | Sobriety in use, customer awareness (wash at 30°C), **economy of functionality** (renting rather than selling), second-hand market, closed-loop recycling |
| VI. Environmental compensation (transversal) | Only for unavoidable impacts: certified carbon credits for sequestration (reforestation, mangroves) or avoidance, biodiversity compensation |

### Social and societal aspects, based on ISO 26000

These subjects are not always handled by the CSR teams: they can be dealt with by HR, General Management or Internal Communication.

| Axis | Levers |
| --- | --- |
| I. Governance & business ethics | Employee representatives on the board, stakeholder committees, parity on the Comex, executive bonuses indexed to social criteria. Anti-corruption charters and training (**Sapin 2 Law**), whistleblower protection, tax transparency |
| II. Purchasing & supplier relations | Audits against forced and child labour, **living wage**, freedom of association, compliance with payment deadlines, fair multi-year contracts |
| III. Human capital & HR practices | Zero occupational accidents, psychosocial risks (PSR), right to disconnect. Pay equality, disability, seniors, young people. Wealth sharing. Continuing education (in the face of AI and the ecological transition), social dialogue with the CSE |
| IV. Consumers & product responsibility | Removal of controversial substances even if legal, no greenwashing or social washing, GDPR, solidarity tariffs, universal accessibility |
| V. Communities & territories | Local employment, work-study students, skills sponsorship, corporate foundation, social and solidarity economy (SSE) projects |

### The 4 levels of CSR maturity

There is no single, universally mandatory grid. The de facto benchmarks are the CSRD and ISO 26000.

1. **Defensive (or Compliance)**: the company simply respects the law to avoid fines or bad buzz. CSR is seen as a constraint.
2. **Corrective (or Optimisation)**: it reduces its direct costs (energy, waste) and carries out occasional sponsorship actions.
3. **Strategic**: CSR is fully integrated into the overall strategy; operations and the supply chain adapt to stakeholders' expectations.
4. **Transformative (or Regenerative)**: CSR drives the business model, with a **positive net impact**.

### The Shein example (group work: moving a company from Defensive to Strategic)

The structure of the assignment, reusable in the exam:

1. Definition of the scope.
2. The regulatory framework.
3. The ISO 26000 analysis.
4. A best-in-class benchmark.
5. A roadmap of 3 concrete, measurable and realistic actions.

Applied to Shein, raw material procurement:

- **Framework**: the EU **REACH** regulation (toxic chemicals) and the **AGEC law** in France (environmental labelling, ban on destroying unsold goods).
- **Diagnosis**: mostly virgin polyester derived from oil. **Defensive** level: chemical tests only serve to avoid seizures at customs.
- **Benchmark**: Patagonia (100% organic cotton, polyester recycled from bottles) and Veja.
- **Actions**: 50% GRS-certified recycled polyester within 2 years, publication of the list of Tier 2 and 3 suppliers, a collection scheme for used clothing recycled into new fibres.

## Session 5 – The regenerative economy

Classic CSR does "less harm"; regenerative CSR does "more good". To get there, a company must move from weak sustainability to strong sustainability and transform its business model, not just its operations.

### Weak sustainability and strong sustainability

| | Weak sustainability | Strong sustainability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Representation | Three overlapping circles, People, Planet, Profit, on the same level | Nested circles: Profit inside People, People inside Planet |
| Principle | Natural capital and human capital are **substitutable**: the total stock just must not fall | Natural capital has **irreplaceable** functions and must be preserved for its own sake |
| Assumptions | Technology replaces nature, growth offsets degradation, nature can be priced (cost-benefit analysis) | Non-substitutable assets, ecological limits, the precautionary principle |
| Criticism or support | Ignores thresholds and irreversibility (extinctions, tipping points), can deplete **critical natural capital** (clean air, a stable climate) | Supported by **ecological economics** (planetary boundaries) |

Line to remember: **no planet, no people; no people, no profit.**

The ecological and social transition requires deep, systemic and complex changes. Example quoted: the clothing brand **Loom** (Julia Faure, TEDx talk on redefining business success).

### The regenerative company

**Definition**: its activity generates more benefits for ecosystems and society than it consumes. It does not just aim for neutrality: it works like a **tree**, which purifies its environment simply by existing.

| | Classic CSR (levels 2 and 3) | Regenerative CSR (level 4) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logic | Offset and reduce: limit the damage | Restore and co-evolve: heal the system |
| Example slogan | "I pollute 20% more slowly and I plant three trees" | "My factory cleans the local water, recreates biodiversity and revitalises the neighbourhood" |

**The 4 pillars**:

1. **Aim for a net positive impact**: non-financial indicators (carbon captured, species returning, local well-being). Prosperity rather than growth.
2. **Learn from living systems (biomimicry)**: in nature, waste does not exist. The company works in a closed loop.
3. **A systemic vision**: the company is an organ of its territory. If the territory is unwell, it helps it, and it cooperates with its ecosystem.
4. **Shared governance**: value is redistributed to employees, suppliers and local ecological projects, not only to shareholders.

### The 4 steps to becoming regenerative (CEC, Convention des Entreprises pour le Climat)

1. **Understand its pressures**: which planetary boundaries are affected, which resources are essential, which social issues, what performance dynamics and what effect growth has on impacts.
2. **Question the essential need** the company meets. Example: a truck maker that only thinks about decarbonising its fleet forgets biodiversity. The French road network has grown by more than 12% in 20 years and the number of heavy goods vehicles by 56%. The real issue is therefore to **reduce the number of trucks and kilometres**.
3. **Set a new course for 2030**: ambitious and unifying. Sobriety, decarbonisation, adaptation, regeneration, cooperation, social justice. The volume logic must be challenged.
4. **Identify 3 to 5 levers of redirection** and the action plans: rethink the offer (renunciation, eco-design, circularity), move to the **economy of functionality**, influence the ecosystem, cooperate, transform management, regenerate soils and ecosystems.

### The three case studies of the course

**Mustela (Laboratoires Expanscience)**: €336 million of revenue, 1,245 employees, a family company present in more than 100 countries.

- Purpose: helping individuals shape their well-being. The **impACT 2030** strategy aims for a positive-impact company with a regenerative ambition, with 4 objectives: eco-socio-designed products, climate and biodiversity, employees, and mobilising communities.
- **Flagship actions**: **stopping baby wipes**, which accounted for **20% of revenue in France**. Marketing that strengthens parents' autonomy. 100% sea freight and a biomass boiler. **Cooperation with direct competitors** (A-Derma, Klorane, Garancia, La Rosée…) to test bulk sales in pharmacies.

**Interface** (the leader in carpet tiles): USD 1 billion of revenue a year, 3,500 employees, 6 production sites.

- **Mission Zero** launched in **1994**, aiming for zero negative impact by 2020. Results: **−95% GHG** and **−94% carpet sent to landfill**.
- It illustrates the full sequence:
    - **Renounce**: 100% renewable energy, glue-free installation (TacTiles), the end of PVC, the QUEST programme against waste.
    - **Reduce**: ReEntry recycling, **Floor as a Service** (renting tiles), 60% biosourced or recycled materials, the bio-inspired i2 design.
    - **Offset**: agroforestry and certified carbon credits.
    - **Regenerate**: **carbon-negative** carpet tiles (CQuest Bio and BioX), "factories as forests", and **Net-Works**, which recycles discarded fishing nets into carpet.

**Renault Trucks**:

- Context: each French person has 27 tonnes transported over 200 km a year. Freight accounts for 9% of French CO2, nearly 90% of freight goes by road, and transport is the only sector whose emissions have not fallen since 1990.
- The new 2030 course, in 4 levers:
    1. The energy transition: electrification, with biofuels as a transition.
    2. Logistics productivity: over 80% utilisation and over 70% fill rate, multimodal transport, cyclo-logistics, **Truck as a Service**.
    3. The circular economy: extending truck life, converting existing trucks to electric, reusing parts.
    4. The territorial transition: the network of 300 service points and shorter supply chains.
- The CEO's message, Christophe Martin: **sell fewer trucks**.

## Answer structure for a CSR case study

1. The company's **value chain**, from upstream to end of life.
2. **Diagnosis**: impacts on planetary boundaries and social floors (the donut), then the ISO 26000 table with the 7 central questions, a level for each question and evidence.
3. **Maturity level**: Defensive, Corrective, Strategic or Transformative.
4. Applicable **legal framework**: CSRD, duty of vigilance, AGEC, REACH, Sapin 2, PACTE.
5. **Risks** (physical, transition, reputational) and **opportunities**.
6. **Action plan**: the avoid, reduce, offset, regenerate sequence, on each link in the chain. 3 measurable actions, with a best-in-class benchmark (Patagonia, Interface, Mustela).
