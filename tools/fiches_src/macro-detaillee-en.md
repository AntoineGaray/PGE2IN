## The course's vision: the common thread to keep in mind

Mr Zumer sets out his method from the second slide: rely on **very well-known authors, classical and above all contemporary**, use **mainly contemporary theories**, **observe and comment on graphs**, and **read the economic press**. The common thread of both chapters, in the professor's own words: **the primacy of institutions**. This is the "Rodrikian" or "de Sotian" idea: **the quality of institutions determines the quality of economic outcomes**. "We get the results that our institutions allow us to get."

- **Braudel**: "A country has the economic situation of its structures."
- **Structural reform**, national and European, is at the heart of today's economic questions.
- The second common thread is the **social contract**: if the wealth created by capitalism only benefits a minority, the system will be fought (de Soto, Rodrik, Polanyi). It appears in chapter 1 (the poor excluded from property) and chapter 2 (the end of Fordism and the new inequalities).

## Chapter 1 – The mysteries of economic development

*Course subtitle: "A certain role for the State, in globalisation…". Reference book: Hernando de Soto, *The Mystery of Capital* (2000).*

**The professor's outline**: 1. Presentation of Hernando de Soto and the mystery of development. 2. Illustrations: Peru, Haiti, the Philippines, Egypt. 3. The debate: legalising property, a necessary but not sufficient condition. 4. Conclusion: globalisation for all and the need for a social contract (Rodrik), with four extensions (France, Europe, the Arab Springs, the subprime crisis).

### 1. Who is Hernando de Soto?

- A **Peruvian liberal** economist, born in **1941 in Arequipa**. The son of a diplomat, he moved to Switzerland aged 7 (1948) and studied at the Graduate Institute of International Studies in Geneva.
- Career: the **GATT**, the Swiss Bank Corporation, **governor of Peru's central bank**. He returned to Peru in 1980 to work with Fernando Belaúnde's government.
- In **1980, in Lima**, he founded the **ILD** (Institute for Liberty and Democracy), which promotes the reforms that allow poor countries to develop. Thirty countries invited him; the heads of state of **more than 35 countries** have called on the ILD. His work inspired **USAID** and the **World Bank**.
- Two major books, translated into more than 20 languages: ***The Other Path*** (1986), a title that answers the **Shining Path** terrorists, who had tried to assassinate him; ***The Mystery of Capital*** (2000): "Why Capitalism Triumphs in the West and Fails Everywhere Else".
- In politics: candidate in Peru's **2021** presidential election (the liberal Avanza País party), he came **4th with 11.6%**. He was also part of Keiko Fujimori's campaign team (2011 and 2016).
- In **2014**, he challenged **Thomas Piketty** (*Capital in the Twenty-First Century*): his statistics would ignore the **90% of the world's population** living in developing and former Soviet countries, where capital is produced and held in the **informal sector**. This is the subject of **document no. 6** (section 8).

### 2. The starting point: the mystery of development

**The finding.** Capitalism is not perfect, but it is **the only system that has created so much wealth**; competing systems have failed in history (the **Maddison curve** over two centuries shows it). Yet in many developing countries, there are only **"pockets of wealth" in an "ocean of poverty"**. Why does capitalism work for some peoples and not for others?

**Braudel's quotation** (*Civilization and Capitalism*, vol. 2: *The Wheels of Commerce*), which opens the chapter: the key problem is to understand why a capitalist sector lived **"in a closed system, even encysted"**, unable to conquer the whole of society, with strong capital formation in some sectors only. This is the image of **the bell jar**: capitalism exists, but under a bell jar, reserved for a minority.

**The false explanations de Soto rejects.**

- The **Washington Consensus**: stabilise the currency, liberalise, privatise, ignore social unrest. These policies **mostly failed** in poor countries.
- Instead of questioning the remedy, Westerners often blamed the peoples: lack of entrepreneurial spirit, of commercial sense, of innovation. It is an almost **"racialist"** argument: capitalism would come from a **Protestant ethic** (Max Weber) and mentalities would block development.
- **De Soto's answer**: in the world's big cities there are **hundreds of thousands of entrepreneurs** who do not lack talent. The problem is not cultural.

**The method: economists must go into the field** (like sociologists and anthropologists; Coase said so too). De Soto himself set up a **small textile business** in the favelas of Lima, with **two sewing machines** bought out of his own pocket. It quickly became profitable. But when he wanted to make it grow:

- **207 operations** and **2 years full-time** to obtain an official existence;
- **289 days at 6 hours a day** to register a business with **a single employee**;
- a cost of **$1,230**, i.e. **31 times the monthly minimum wage** of the country.

### 3. The illustrations: the obstacle course (Peru, the Philippines, Egypt, Haiti)

The PowerPoint shows the **procedure flowcharts** from the book: grids covered with arrows going in every direction. This is the image to describe in the exam.

The procedure to legalise a dwelling has 5 phases; the first alone requires **207 operations**. **90%** of Lima's small industrial businesses are illegal; **85%** of urban transport and **60%** of food distribution are provided by non-legalised businesses. Legalising a plot: **13 to 25 years** |

| **Egypt** | Registering a plot: **5 to 14 years** of full-time procedures (the slide: 6 to 14 years for desert land intended for building). Building legally on agricultural land: **6 to 12 years**. More than **90%** of city and rural dwellers without a title |
| **Haiti** | Settling legally: lease the land for **5 years**, with **65 formalities** taking **2 years**. In total, **19 years** to legalise acquired land. **67%** of city dwellers and **more than 80%** of rural dwellers without a title |
| **Haiti (Port-au-Prince)** | The slide estimates the **value of informal buildings** in Port-au-Prince, house by house: from small shacks to multi-storey buildings. The message: these assets are worth something, but nobody holds the title |
| **France** | **Permit A38** (*The Twelve Tasks of Asterix*): "the place that sends you mad". Bureaucracy exists here too, but in a comic version, not to the point of blocking the whole economy |

**De Soto's line to use**: these people's illegality is not a **lack of respect for the law** on their part. It is rather **the opposite**: it is **the State that does not respect them**, with such long delays and such heavy formalities.

**The result**: in developing countries, only a **thin slice of the population** benefits from the legal economy. There is no legal system to **guarantee and protect** property.

### 4. The heart of the matter: dead capital

**The surprising finding.** Looking at poor neighbourhoods, de Soto discovered that **people own a lot**: their house, their land, their tools. The cumulative value is huge (Egypt, Haiti); his teams estimated it on the spot, with lists of assets. The poor do not lack wealth: they lack **titles**.

**Dead capital.** Without a reliable legal system, these assets are **static, paralysed, used in a purely local way**. They can neither circulate, nor be invested, nor create additional wealth. Dead capital represents up to **90% of the economy** of some countries.

**What capital is for de Soto** (the course definition):

- a **surplus** in a productive process, **reinvested** to create additional wealth, like a **chain of innovation** (economies of scale, productivity gains);
- something **almost virtual**: it appears when an asset is **represented by a property title**. The title allows it to be accumulated, divided, sold, mortgaged: assets become **fungible**, interchangeable;
- developed countries have managed to **"extract an invisible potential"** from assets. The title **fixes the economic potential** of a house.

**The example that sums it all up**: **70% of new businesses in the United States** are financed by a **bank loan**, itself obtained thanks to a **mortgage** (often on the entrepreneur's house). No title, no mortgage; no mortgage, no credit; no credit, no business.

**Property rights are not only about protection.** Their main use is the **circulation of property rights**, guaranteed by a reliable legal system. **Capital is alive when it circulates.**

**Being virtual is not a flaw.** Many things are symbolic in economics and finance (a title, a share, accounting). For de Soto, capitalism cannot be condemned for being abstract: **the means are abstract, but the wealth created is very real**.

**His major contribution**: he was the **first economist**, in the early 2000s, to take the **extra-legal sector** seriously. For a long time, only the West had this mechanism for converting assets into capital, **without even knowing it**.

> **Everyday life.** Any homeowner's **mortgage**: the bank lends because the house has a **title registered with a notary** and can serve as collateral. This is exactly de Soto's "living" capital. Conversely, something you own without an invoice or papers (a bike, a car without registration) is hard to sell to a stranger: it is a small piece of dead capital.

### 5. Why are poor countries' legal systems failing?

- **The colonial legacy**: colonial law was not designed to **create** wealth, but to **extract** it and protect the colonists' property.
- **Law designed to protect, not to circulate**: yet for de Soto, property rights should be a **right of circulation of capital**.
- **The historical comparison**: 18th-century Europe (the **Colbert** era in France, with its many legal prohibitions) and **19th-century United States** (the **conquest of the West**). Settlers occupied land and federal judges took **more than a century** to unify the rules and give everyone definitive property rights. Today's poor countries are at the same stage.
- A de Soto line to quote: **"poor countries need the solutions that developed countries adopted in the 19th century, not in the 21st"**.

### 6. The debate: legalising property, a necessary but not sufficient condition

This is the exact title of the third part of the professor's outline: **a necessary condition but not a sufficient one**.

**What de Soto proposes.**

- **Go into the field** and **record** all the property rights that already exist, including **customary and local rights**.
- Create, under government authority, **study offices** to build a **new legal system** that founds a **social contract for all**.
- A **gradual protocol**, adapted to each country. The aim is not to check that everything works immediately, but to lay a **minimum foundation** before other reforms.
- The population must **accept** the reform, introduced gradually: it is the only thing that can work. It gives access to a bigger market and **unlocks the housing market**.

**Why it is not enough: the political conditions.**

- **Lawyers**: for de Soto, jurists and lawyers are **the most conservative fringe** of these societies. They have the best tools to block... or unblock. A few **open-minded figures** must be found among them and relied upon.
- **Courageous, reforming heads of state** (this is also **Myerson**'s idea), surrounded by a small team of competent jurists, to avoid **corruption** and reform in depth.
- An **elite on the ground** that understands the urgency of the social contract.
- The final goal: an **economic and legal democracy**. **"Democracy cannot be reduced to voting alone."**

**Concrete results**: the ILD has advised around thirty governments on bringing the extra-legal sector into the legal one. In Peru, the ILD got a law passed in **1991** for the cadastral registration of irregular properties, the **Ley de Registro de Predios Rurales**.

> **Addition.** The **criticisms** of de Soto ("criticisms of this approach" in the professor's outline) are not detailed in the course material. The best known: the **Buenos Aires** experiment (Galiani and Schargrodsky: titles encourage investment in the house and children's education, but unlock little **credit**), **Erica Field**'s work in Peru (titles mainly free up **working time**), the risk of losing **customary rights**. They all support the "necessary but not sufficient" view. Details are in addition C of the French sheet.

### 7. Chapter conclusion: globalisation for all and the need for a social contract

**The social contract according to de Soto.** Like **Rodrik**, de Soto believes that **without a solid social contract, capitalism will collapse**: it will be fought by the people and will not hold with a few pockets of wealth. More **middle classes** and better-shared wealth are needed. For him, **the real social contract is the legal system**: giving individuals legal freedom. One can even write **social rights = legal rights**.

**The references that go with it**:

- **Karl Polanyi**, *The Great Transformation*: if inequalities spread and peoples turn against the market economy, it **paves the way for fascism**.
- **Marx**: de Soto has read him and thinks Marxist thought is **more useful for understanding capitalism** than "mainstream" thought, which no longer knows where capital comes from. But **de Soto is not a Marxist**: he goes further than Marx on the relationship to capital.
- **Samir Amin**: the thinking of those who want to change society is necessarily of higher quality than that of those who want to keep it still.
- **T. S. Eliot**: his famous lines on the wisdom we have lost in knowledge, and the knowledge we have lost in information.

**The four extensions of de Soto's framework** (to know, they make very good examples):

| Extension | De Soto's analysis |
| --- | --- |
| **France** | Permit A38: bureaucracy exists in a rich country too. And 18th-century France (Colbert) looked like today's poor countries |
| **Europe** | Each country has its own legal system (bankruptcy law, civil law...). **No unified legal market, so no real single market.** This **legal fragmentation** limits economies of scale and may explain why no European capitalism rivals American capitalism |
| **The Arab Springs** | In Tunisia, most "revolutionaries" were **small traders without property titles**, exposed to arrests and **arbitrary confiscations**. Article: "The Economic Roots of the Arab Spring" (de Soto). Document no. 6 details the case of **Mohamed Bouazizi** (section 8) |
| **The subprime crisis** | A **property rights crisis**: nobody really knew what the "toxic" securities contained. De Soto speaks of **creating a financial Third World in the West** |

> **Humour.** **Permit A38** is the best possible wink, since the professor put it in the course himself: "To register his workshop in Lima, de Soto lived 289 days in Asterix's place that sends you mad."

### 8. Course document no. 6: "The Poor Versus Piketty"

A text handed out in class: Hernando de Soto answers Thomas Piketty's bestseller, *Capital in the Twenty-First Century*. It refers to Charlie Hebdo, so it probably dates from 2015. It is the developed version of the criticism of Piketty mentioned in de Soto's biography in the PowerPoint.

**What Piketty says (the thesis under attack)**

- *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2013 in French, 2014 in English), a worldwide success.
- Over the 19th and 20th centuries, the return on capital (r) is generally higher than the growth rate of the economy (g): **r > g**.
- What the formula means: if wealth yields more than the economy grows, those who already own capital get richer faster than those who live from their work. Inequalities widen **by themselves**, and inheritance weighs more and more. According to Piketty, this leads to poverty, violence and war.
- His solutions: a **global progressive tax on capital** and a global **"financial cadastre"** recording who owns what.

**De Soto's answer, in 4 steps**

1. **A data error**: Piketty only looks at rich countries' statistics. Yet **90% of the world's population** lives in developing or former Soviet countries, where capital is **informal**, hence invisible. He applies European categories ("employee", "unemployed", "capitalist") to the rest of the world: a **Eurocentric** analysis.
2. **The Egyptian evidence**: at the request of Egypt's finance ministry, the ILD mobilised **120 researchers** to compare statistics with the field. **47%** of so-called "labour" income actually comes from capital (**$20 billion** of wages, plus **$18 billion** of informal capital income). Egyptians own nearly **$360 billion** of real estate, i.e. **8 times** all foreign investment received since Napoleon's expedition. The same man is both a capitalist and a worker.
3. **The Arab revolutions, revolts for capital**: **Mohamed Bouazizi** (Tunisia, December 2010), presented as "unemployed", had been **an entrepreneur since the age of 12** and wanted to accumulate capital (*ras el-mel*). Within the **next two months**, **63 other entrepreneurs** attempted suicide in public; **four governments** fell. The ILD interviewed almost half of the **37 survivors**: all had been driven to despair by the **expropriation** of their small capital. About **300 million** Arabs live in the same conditions.
4. **Fictitious capital in the West**: de Soto agrees with Piketty on the **lack of transparency** since 2008. But a financial cadastre would record **paper with no real value** (derivatives backed by poorly documented assets): this is **fictitious capital** (an expression of Marx and Jefferson). His solution: link each piece of capital to its owner in a **formal document**, like a property title. His model: **revolutionary France**, which created a new system for registering titles; he quotes the 19th-century economist **Charles Coquelin**.

**De Soto's 4 lessons**

1. It is not capital that causes poverty and violence, but its **absence**.
2. Outside the West, capital and labour are not enemies: they are **two sides of the same value creation**.
3. The main obstacle for the poorest is their **inability to accumulate and protect their capital**.
4. Respect for the individual and the courage to speak out are not reserved for the West.

**The key line**: in the West, **"paper without assets"**; everywhere else, **"assets without paper"**.

| | **Piketty** | **De Soto** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Capital is...** | A source of inequalities that widen by themselves | What the poor seek, and lack |
| **The ground** | Rich countries, over two centuries, with tax statistics | Developing countries, through field surveys |
| **Revolts** | Will come from inequalities of capital | Are already here, and demand access to capital |
| **Capital and labour** | Opposed (rentiers versus wage earners) | Merged (the poor man is also a small capitalist) |
| **The West's problem** | The concentration of wealth | Fictitious capital, paper with no real value |
| **The solution** | Tax capital, a global financial cadastre | Property titles, each piece of capital linked to an owner |

> **Addition.** To qualify (these limits do not come from the text): the two authors are not talking about the same world, and may each be right on their own ground; de Soto's figures come from his own institute; the Arab Spring also had political causes, unemployment among young graduates and rising food prices; for most economists, the 2008 crisis was mainly about financial regulation and debt.

> **Everyday life.** "Paper without assets" is a bit like the balance on a buy-now-pay-later or crypto app: figures on a screen, without always knowing what lies behind them. "Assets without paper" is an inherited house that nobody ever registered with a notary: you live in it, but you can neither sell it easily nor borrow against it.

## Chapter 2 – The mystery of economic growth: how to achieve it? The sources of productivity

*Subtitle in the notes: "Towards a new global economic paradigm?"*

**The professor's outline**: Section 1 – Is there a "new economy"? (definition, the Solow paradox, Gordon and Jesus). Section 2 – The O-ring model and the end of Fordism (O-ring production, globalisation and industrial revolution: the emergence of the South, Angus Maddison, the "Makers"). Section 3 – Conclusion: the end of Fordism?

**The opening graph: the degree of globalisation, 1960-2020.** The curve rises from about **12 in 1960** to nearly **30 in 2008**, falls in **2009** (crisis, about 25.6), climbs back to around **29.5** in the 2010s, then falls back to about **27** in 2019-2020. The unit is not given on the slide: remember the shape (a continuous rise, a plateau since 2008).

### 1. Is there a "new economy"? The definitions

**In the press**: the new economy means the **ICT sector** (technologies linked, closely or loosely, to the computer). Depending on the calculation, **10 to 15% of US GDP** and **5 to 6% of French GDP**.

**Among economists**: the idea that industrialised economies are going through a **third industrial revolution**. Its origin: a technical advance, **computing in the broad sense**. Since the 1990s, the spread of computers would have changed **the way of producing and the very organisation of firms**.

**The stake**: is this revolution comparable to the two previous ones? If so, it will bring new growth and **higher living standards**, as they did. Everything we have gained in living standards comes from the first two industrial revolutions.

**The condition**: it is not enough for computers to be everywhere. There must also be **a change in the organisation of work** within firms.

**Course fact sheet no. 1: the two industrial revolutions**

| Revolution | When and where | Inventions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **First** | Mainly in England, around **1760-1830** | The steam engine, the railway, the steam-powered loom |
| **Second** | Europe and the United States, around **1860-1900** | The **internal combustion engine** (1877: the car, motorised transport, the Wright brothers' plane in 1903), **electricity** (1879: lighting, electric motors, washing machines, refrigeration, air conditioning), chemistry and medicines, the **telegraph** (1844), the **telephone** (1876), the phonograph (1877), mass photography (1880s-1890s), **radio** (1899), cinema (1891 Edison, 1896 the Lumière brothers), **television** (1911, mainly after 1931 and the war), **running water at home** (from 1880), **Pasteur** (1880, the start of falling mortality), reinforced concrete (1877) |

**The golden age of productivity: 1913-1972** (fact sheet no. 1).

**Angus Maddison** (1926-2010, British economist and historian, University of Groningen) reconstructed **very long-run GDP per capita** series, from year 1 to 1998, for every region of the world. On his **semi-logarithmic** graphs, the take-off of living standards coincides with the two industrial revolutions. They are what produced technical progress.

### 2. The Solow paradox (1987)

**The line**: **"You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics"**: computers are everywhere, except in **labour productivity** statistics (New York Times Book Review, July 1987).

**The reasoning**: if the computer is a real technological revolution, it must **make work more efficient**. Otherwise it is just an improved tool, nothing more. Yet **labour productivity and living standards are linked**: a real 3rd industrial revolution must show up in productivity.

**The context**: very strong and uninterrupted US growth that economists did not understand. At first they put it down to luck (falling raw material and oil prices, a stimulus policy mix). Then, after 10 or 15 years, they wondered whether a major innovation was spreading. But labour productivity was not moving.

**The end of the paradox**: from the **2000s**, US statistics finally show a rise in productivity. In **2000**, Solow himself declared that computers had **definitely entered the statistics**. Between **1995 and 2005**, US growth averaged **4% a year**, on a rising profile with strong productivity gains.

**Why it is the foundation of the debate**: if the paradox disappears, a 3rd industrial revolution is under way, capable of raising living standards like the two previous ones.

### 3. Gordon and Jesus: the sceptic

**Robert Gordon** (born 1940, Northwestern University) disputes the idea of a 3rd industrial revolution. His arguments:

1. **The optical illusion (averaging effect)**: productivity would only have risen **in the ICT sector itself**, because making ICT uses other ICT intensively. The average gives the impression that the whole economy is progressing.
2. **Diminishing returns**: more computing power does not mean more productivity. His graph *Diminishing Returns in the New Economy*: total utility rises quickly (from the memory typewriter to WordPerfect 4.2), then levels off (WP 6.0, the latest Word), while MHz keep rising.
3. **The marginal utility of computers falls quickly.**
4. **The internet**: it has mainly served to **distribute** products and cut their costs, not to create them. And at the office, employees use it... for something else: **30 to 40%** of employees' online activity is unrelated to the company, and **15%** of US employees look for a new job during working hours. The course's **2015 Olfeo study** (5 European countries): **1 h 53 a day** on the internet, of which **44%** personal browsing, i.e. **50 minutes a day** and **25 days a year**.
5. **The telegraph did better**: it multiplied transmission speed by **3,000** and divided the cost by **100**. Before it, sending a message from New York to Chicago took **10 days**.

**His conclusion**: if there was a rise in productivity, it already happened 15 years ago. He advises investing in the **education system and universities** instead.

**Why "Gordon and Jesus"?** For Gordon, those who believe in the 3rd industrial revolution are **waiting for a messiah** (new technologies) who will not come.

**Document no. 12: the Gordon interview** (*Le Monde*, 20/02/2016, on the release of *The Rise and Fall of American Growth*): "**The golden age of growth is behind us**".

- Strong US growth from **1870 to 1970** was an **exceptional parenthesis**: running water, electricity, the telephone, the car, medicine.
- Since 1970, **total factor productivity** has been weakening.
- The 3rd industrial revolution only concerns a **narrow sphere** (entertainment, information and communication), worth **7% of US GDP**. At the office, the great leap took place in the late 1990s; since then, a PC, a phone, a connection: **the major breakthroughs have already happened**.
- It is **not a measurement problem**: the reach of innovations has always been underestimated.
- Jobs are not threatened: transformations are slow, the economy has time to adapt.
- The **American dream** has taken a hit: inequality, eroding real incomes, blocked social mobility.

**Document no. 11: Krugman versus Gordon** ("Gordon Versus The Androids", *New York Times*, 7 December 2013). For Krugman, Gordon is **persuasive but probably wrong**.

- Gordon's argument as Krugman summarises it: the digital revolution does not match the great innovations of the late 19th century. **"Indoor plumbing beats iPads."**
- The reply: imagine real **androids**, able to do almost everything humans do. Diminishing returns to capital would disappear: raising GDP per capita would just mean **multiplying androids**.
- And **"something has happened"**: speech recognition, machine translation and self-driving cars have become reality, thanks to **big data and correlations** ("mindless algorithms"). If they replace humans, it does not matter that they do not think.
- So one can be **optimistic about growth** and **pessimistic about wages**. "Also, Skynet will kill us all."

**What most economists (and the professor) think**: Gordon forgets all the **uses** of ICT. They feed into every science and trigger other innovations. **A Covid vaccine within a year would have been unthinkable without computers.** Almost all economists think we have entered a new era, with a change in the organisation of firms.

**The total factor productivity graph** (OECD, base 100 in 1991): in 2011, about **127 in the United States**, **121 in Germany**, **117 in France**, but only **104 in Spain** and **103 in Italy**. Countries do not all benefit equally from the technological revolution.

> **Everyday life.** The Solow paradox, student version: a laptop in class can make note-taking twice as fast... or be used to watch videos. Technology only makes you productive if **the organisation** follows. That is exactly what the course says.

> **Humour.** "Gordon waits for the 3rd industrial revolution the way others wait for the Messiah: he does not believe it will come." Or, with Krugman: "Indoor plumbing beats the iPad, but the iPad did help write this paper."

### 4. The "net economy": the ICT sector

**What it includes**: computing, electronics, micro-electronics, telecoms, audiovisual and multimedia. It must be measured as a whole.

- About **15% of the US economy** (a rising share), about **8% of the French economy**. The gap comes mainly from **components** (chips, microprocessors).
- **Europe** has fallen far behind over the past 15 years, not only the **United States** but also **China** and **Korea**, which did not miss the turn.
- The sector employs more and more people: it is no longer a marginal sector.
- The **Lisbon summit** (2000) aimed to make Europe, within ten years, **"the most competitive knowledge-based economy in the world"**.

> **Addition.** The course notes say "North Korea": it is clearly **South Korea** (Samsung, semiconductors).

**Should a country produce ICT?** In theory, there is no point producing what is not one of your **comparative advantages**. But this sector has **very high value added**: not being in it is worrying. Producing countries earn a **rent**:

- the giant profits of the sector's leaders;
- these profits, through shares held by households, act as a **macroeconomic shock absorber** in times of crisis, especially for the American middle class;
- they are **reinvested in research**, creating a **virtuous circle** and new companies;
- the producing country knows these products and their uses in other sectors better.

This is the idea of the **first mover advantage**. The ICT sector is key because **it feeds all the others**. The **United States** is the country that benefits most from this rent.

### 5. A new production regime

**The characteristics of ICT goods** (intrinsic characteristics):

1. **Sunk investments**: high start-up costs, not recoverable in case of failure.
2. **Strong economies of scale**: most of the cost is in **design** (fixed costs); variable costs are low or zero (example: software). Large quantities must be produced to recover the costs.
3. **Network effects**: a product's usefulness rises with **the number of its users**. **Metcalfe's law**: the value of a network rises with the **square** of the number of its users.

**The consequence**: these two effects naturally create **monopolies**. The sector has high **barriers to entry**: countries that missed the start will find it very hard to catch up.

**The support of a financial revolution in the United States**:

- **capital markets** finance start-ups: American households hold more shares and fewer physical assets, and this money is available for young companies;
- the **unicorn** phenomenon (start-ups valued at more than **one billion dollars**);
- researchers leave a large company to found their start-up, which can eliminate the old competitors;
- the game of **acquisitions** (Microsoft is one of the biggest buyers of start-ups) makes research profitable;
- **partnerships between the State, universities and companies**.

**In France**: an **insufficient** research effort (State and business R&D spending), a widening gap. One attempt: the **regional competitiveness clusters** (*pôles de compétitivité*), under Villepin.

**The heart of the new economy: a new organisation of work.** Computers are not only used to be more efficient, but to **organise work differently**:

- greater adaptability to demand;
- greater responsiveness of firms;
- greater versatility of employees;
- delegation of responsibilities to lower hierarchical levels;
- great flexibility of production;
- networking of production units, inside and outside the firm.

**The conclusion**: **massive and widespread use of subcontracting**.

> **Everyday life.** **Network effects**: a class WhatsApp group is useless if you are alone in it, and becomes essential when the whole year group is in it. Same for the class revision site: the more players there are, the more interesting the leaderboard. Metcalfe live.

### 6. The O-ring model (Michael Kremer)

**The author**: **Michael Kremer** (born 1964), professor at **Harvard**, **Nobel Prize in Economics 2019**; his research covers education, health, water and agriculture in developing countries. The model dates from the **1990s**; it is what **Silicon Valley** management knows.

**The origin of the name**: an **O-ring** is a **seal**. A **US space shuttle**, built by around a hundred companies, was lost because of **a single part**: the weak link in a long chain of subcontractors.

> **Addition.** It was the shuttle **Challenger**, which exploded in **January 1986** because of a faulty seal. The name is not in the course material.

**The lesson**: in a production chain, **the slightest malfunction in a single part threatens everything**. The skill level of the workers in the chain must therefore be **close, homogeneous**. An error at a nuclear subcontractor is extremely costly.

**Why computing changes everything**:

- it makes it possible to combine **more decentralised and more numerous** manufacturing processes; supply chains are longer and more specialised than ever;
- assembly is **more flexible** than in the age of mass production, hence **more sensitive**;
- firms have many subcontractors, sometimes **subcontractors of subcontractors**: this is the **fragmentation of production**, a division of labour pushed to the extreme;
- this model is only possible if **large amounts of data can be transmitted instantly over a network** (the computer and the internet). Without that, the only possible organisation remains the **pyramid**.

**The course's two diagrams** (to be able to redraw):

| | **Old economy: Fordist organisation** | **New economy: the O-ring model** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shape** | Three separate **pyramids** (firms E1, E2, E3): management, executives, engineers, employees, factories, all in the **North** | A **network**: each head office in the North is linked to subcontractors, accounting, factories and call centres spread between the North, the **South** and the **very poor countries** |
| **The South** | Question marks: **no place** in industrial production | Factories, subcontractors, call centres: the South **enters the chain** |
| **Example** | **Renault in Boulogne-Billancourt**: the big integrated factory | The **Boeing 787 Dreamliner**, the **Falcon 7X**, the **Volvo S40** |
| **Why** | The pyramid was the most rational organisation for **transmitting information** | The network allows **huge productivity gains**: there is no going back |

**The course illustrations**:

- **The Boeing 787 Dreamliner puzzle**: parts made by **North American, Asian and European** suppliers (wings in Nagoya, Japan; centre fuselage in Grottaglie, Italy; passenger doors in Toulouse; General Electric or Rolls-Royce engines; wing tips in Seoul...).
- **The Volvo S40**: **23 components** from many countries. A car's value added comes from **at least ten countries**; according to the WTO and the OECD, **between a third and a half** of components and transport equipment are imported, reworked and re-exported.
- **Dassault Aviation's Falcon 7X** (document no. 15, *01 Informatique*, 2004): unheard of in aeronautics, partners **spread all over the world** designed the aircraft together, **remotely**, on a **virtual platform**.

**Selective (assortative) matching**: each production unit becomes a **small homogeneous group** of workers with almost identical skills and training (a clustering, agglomeration phenomenon).

> **Everyday life.** The O-ring model is **group work** at school: if a single member does not hand in their part, the whole group's mark collapses. That is also why people try to team up with others at their level: selective matching.

### 7. A new type of inequality

- **Small differences in performance** produce **huge differences in income**, often incomprehensible from the outside.
- The course example: two **IT engineers** from the **same school**, with the same skills. One works on a big project (a space shuttle), the other as an engineer at **Carrefour**: their pay has nothing in common.
- The firm no longer protects its employees as it did after the war. Anyone can be **excluded from a production chain**. **Careers become more uncertain**.
- The danger: an **explosion of inequality within each group** (same age, same degree, same social category).
- **The struggle changes nature: from a struggle between classes to a struggle within classes** (intra-class, or within-group, inequality).

> **Everyday life.** Two graduates from the same year, with the same grades, can have very different starting salaries depending on the firm and the project they land on. This is the intra-class inequality of the O-ring model.

### 8. Industrial revolution and globalisation: the emergence of the South

**The most important idea of the chapter: it is the industrial revolution that made globalisation possible, not the other way round.** Just as the first industrial revolution preceded the first globalisation.

- The fragmentation of the production chain (the **supply chain**) creates **niches** that poor countries can slip into: computers, printers or hard drives can be produced in Asia.
- Before, everything was concentrated in the big factories of rich countries (the pyramid, Fordist model): **only Japan** had made the transition.
- It is a **paradigm shift**, a **mutation of capitalism**, which opened up North-South relations.

**The flawed reasoning to denounce**: starting from **globalisation** to explain the difficulties of rich countries. This error feeds **protectionism**, which would shatter the hopes of poor countries. For them, **trade** is the hope of narrowing wealth gaps that come entirely from the last two centuries and the two industrial revolutions.

**The great hope of the 21st century**: that the gap between a small group of rich countries and the rest of the world **closes from the top**, with the emergence of **middle classes** in poor countries.

**The course's Maddison graphs** ("When and Why did the West get Richer than the Rest?"):

- **China and Western Europe, 400 to 1998**: similar GDP per capita for centuries; Europe takes off with the industrial revolution, China stagnates then climbs back at the end of the period.
- **China versus the United Kingdom and versus the United States, 1700-1998**: the gap widens for two centuries, before China's catch-up.
- **GDP per capita in England since 1270**: almost flat for 500 years, then an explosion from 1800.
- **World GDP over two millennia**: almost nothing until 1800, then a vertical rise to over $100,000 billion.
- **GDP per capita of the UK, Japan, Italy, China and India**: the UK goes first, Japan and Italy catch up in the 20th century, China and India take off at the very end.

> **Everyday life.** A smartphone or laptop often says "Designed in California, assembled in China": designed in the North, assembled in the South, with components from a dozen countries. That is the O-ring model and the emergence of the South in your pocket.

### 9. The "Makers": towards a 4th industrial revolution?

- **Chris Anderson**'s book, *Makers: The New Industrial Revolution* (**2012**). Some speak of a **4th industrial revolution**.
- The innovation in question: **3D printers**. Production could become **even more decentralised** than in the O-ring model.
- Originally, Silicon Valley start-ups organised in **FabLabs**, to free themselves from the industrial world, from Fordism and even from the O-ring model.
- On **12 February 2013**, in his State of the Union address, **Barack Obama** said 3D printing has the potential to **revolutionise the way we make almost everything**. He announced funding for centres so that this revolution would be **"made in America"**.
- The course shows a **FabLab in Paris (13th arrondissement)**: Les Ingénieux.

**The professor's finding**: the more a country engages in the new economy (innovation, R&D in future-oriented sectors), the higher its **GDP per capita** and the lower its **youth unemployment**. The industrial revolution **keeps renewing itself**. Countries that fall behind risk being **downgraded**, like those that missed the previous revolutions.

### 10. Conclusion: the end of Fordism?

**Fordism**: a link between **productivity and wages**. Productivity was rising (Fordism was itself an industrial revolution). To retain their workforce, firms such as **Ford** raised wages. Part of the **productivity gains** was passed on to workers, thanks in particular to **trade unions** (hence the importance **Keynesians** give them).

**The end of the big Fordist factory**:

- it **opened up North-South relations** and allowed the countries of the South to emerge;
- it **weakens the post-war social contract**: without Fordism, no longer the same social protection. The decline mainly hits **unskilled work**.

**Rodrik's contribution**: the social contract must be **preserved and renewed**, adapted to the **new inequalities** of the O-ring model and globalisation.

**Productivity rests on institutions** (back to the common thread): the overall organisation of the economy, the **absence of waste** (inefficiency, corruption), the proper use of public money, and above all the **quality of economic institutions**. It is a kind of **economic democracy**: a country can have democratic structures and still work badly economically.

**The last slide**: the opening credits of *Spellbound* (Hitchcock, 1945), which quote Shakespeare: **"The fault... is not in our stars, but in ourselves"**. In other words: our economic destiny depends on our choices and our institutions, not on fate.

> **Key point.** The conclusion of chapter 2 joins that of chapter 1: **institutions and the social contract decide whether growth benefits everyone**. It is the most natural crossover between the two subjects.

## Exam method, following Mr Zumer's instructions

**What is expected**: no compulsory introduction, no detailed outline. A **chain of well-argued reasoning**, each step supported by an example from the course and, if possible, an example from everyday life or personal experience. A touch of humour is allowed.

### The reasoning block (repeat 4 to 6 times)

1. **The idea** in one sentence, linked to the subject.
2. **The mechanism**: why, through which chain of causes? ("because... therefore...")
3. **The course example**: an author, a figure, a graph, a document (de Soto in Lima, Solow 1987, the Boeing 787...).
4. **The everyday-life or personal example**: what makes the paper come alive and shows you have understood.
5. **The limit or the link to the next idea**: this is what creates the chain. A crossover can serve as a transition.

### Timing in 1h30

| Time | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| **5 min** | Read both subjects and choose. The right subject is the one for which you have the most course examples |
| **10 min** | On rough paper: list 4 to 6 ideas in a logical order, with a course example and a personal example for each. Spot a crossover |
| **65 min** | Write the blocks, taking care over transitions. One or two opening sentences are enough to get into the subject |
| **10 min** | A short conclusion (a quotation from the course works very well), then check names, dates and figures |

### The touch of humour: how much

- **One or two** per paper, no more. It must **serve an argument**, not replace it.
- The best come **from the course itself**: Permit A38, "Gordon and Jesus", "Indoor plumbing beats iPads", "Skynet will kill us all", employees shopping online at the office.
- Never humour at the expense of poor populations: de Soto presents them as entrepreneurs, not as victims.

### Possible subjects and the chain of reasoning

*These are hypotheses built from the course outline, not announced subjects.*

**Chapter 1 – "Why do poor countries stay poor?"**

1. It is not a question of culture or mentality: entrepreneurs are everywhere (against Weber and the Washington Consensus).
2. It is the cost of legality that excludes them (Lima: 289 days, 31 minimum wages; Haiti: 19 years).
3. Their assets become dead capital: they own, but cannot borrow (70% of US businesses are born from a mortgage).
4. The root cause is institutional: colonial law designed to extract, conservative lawyers.
5. Legalisation is necessary but not sufficient: courageous leaders and a social contract are needed.
6. Crossover: even with titles, a country must still enter the new economy to grow (chapter 2).

**Chapter 1 – "Can capitalism benefit everyone?"**: the wealth created (Maddison), the bell jar (Braudel), dead capital, the social contract (Rodrik, Polanyi), the Arab Springs and the subprimes as proof by failure.

**Chapter 1 – "Is capital the cause of inequality or the cure for poverty?"**: Piketty's thesis (r > g), then de Soto's answer in document no. 6 (Egypt, Bouazizi, the 4 lessons, fictitious capital), dead capital and the need for a social contract. Possible crossover: the intra-class inequalities of the O-ring model.

**Chapter 2 – "Is there a new economy?"**

1. What a 3rd industrial revolution would be: a technology plus a new organisation of work.
2. The Solow paradox (1987) and its disappearance in the 2000s.
3. Gordon's arguments: the averaging effect, diminishing returns, the internet at the office, 1870-1970 as a golden parenthesis.
4. Krugman's and most economists' answer: uses, AI, the Covid vaccine.
5. The real proof: the new organisation of production (O-ring, Boeing 787, Falcon 7X).
6. The consequences: emergence of the South, new inequalities, the end of Fordism. Crossover: institutions decide who benefits.

**Chapter 2 – "Is globalisation to blame for the difficulties of rich countries?"**: the flawed reasoning according to the course (the industrial revolution comes before globalisation), the O-ring model, the emergence of the South, the protectionist risk, the end of Fordism and the need for a new social contract (Rodrik).

### Mistakes that cost points

- Reciting the course without **reasoning**: every example must prove something.
- Forgetting the **everyday-life examples**, explicitly requested by the professor.
- Saying that globalisation **causes** the new economy: for the course, it is **the opposite**.
- Confusing **Solow** (the paradox) and **Gordon** (the sceptic), or attributing the O-ring model to anyone other than **Kremer**.
- Presenting de Soto as a Marxist (he is not) or as an enemy of the State (he asks for a State that makes good law).
- Forgetting that **the exam is in English**: see the vocabulary list at the end of the short sheet.
