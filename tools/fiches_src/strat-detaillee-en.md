## Session 1 – What is Strategy? The evolution of a concept

Strategy was born in the military, was formalised by game theory, then became central to business with 20th-century competition. Four great authors structure the course: Andrews, Ansoff, Porter and Mintzberg.

### The origins of strategy

- **Strategy as a military concept**: Sun Tzu, *The Art of War* (4th century BC). The key idea: the aim of war is to force the enemy to give up the fight, even without fighting, thanks to cunning, espionage, great mobility and adaptation to the opponent's strategy. The word comes from the Greek *strategos*, the general in charge of the army, from *stratos* ("army in order of battle") and *ageîn* ("to drive"). Strategy is therefore the art of leading an army.
- **Strategy as a game theory** (20th century): a branch of mathematics and economics that studies decision-making in competitive situations, when each participant's outcome depends on the choices of others (**strategic interaction**).
- **Strategy in a competitive environment** (20th and 21st centuries): three factors made strategic thinking essential. Technological advancements (the internet, mobile devices, automation), globalization (new customer bases and worldwide competition), and shifts in consumer behavior: consumers became more informed, connected and demanding.

### The concept of strategy according to great authors

| Author | Book and date | Central idea | Tools to remember |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Kenneth Andrews** (Harvard) | *The Concept of Corporate Strategy* (1971) | Strategy defines the company's mission, objectives, policies and type of business. It links internal capabilities with the external environment | **LCAG** model (Learned, Christensen, Andrews, Guth), the ancestor of SWOT. Three steps: defining, formulating, implementing strategy |
| **Igor Ansoff** | *Corporate Strategy* (1965) | Strategic planning: diagnosis of competitive positions and diagnosis of the environment | **Ansoff Matrix**, synergy and competency profiles, competitive gap analysis, business portfolio selection, mission concept |
| **Michael Porter** | 1980, 1985, 1990 | Strategy is a combination of the objectives a firm strives to achieve and the means by which it seeks to achieve them, with a long-term allocation of resources | **5 forces** (1980), **value chain** (1985), the **diamond** of national competitiveness (1990) |
| **Henry Mintzberg** | *The Rise and Fall of Strategic Planning*, *Strategy Safari* | Realized strategy is a blend of deliberate and emergent strategy | **Deliberate / emergent** strategy, the **5 Ps**, the 3 fallacies of planning, the **10 schools** |

Andrews highlights two concepts: **long-term development** (clearly defined goals, the "strategic intent" or "deliberate strategy") and **the uniqueness of each company**, which has its own business model and seeks differentiation from competitors. He distinguishes strategy formulation from implementation, but sees them as cyclical and iterative: the outcomes of implementation inform future formulation. The term "Corporate Strategy" was replaced by "Strategic Management" in the mid-1970s.

### The Ansoff Matrix

| | Existing products | New products |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Existing markets** | **Market Penetration**: increase market share (aggressive marketing, promotional campaigns) | **Product Development**: innovate for existing markets |
| **New markets** | **Market Development**: new geographical or demographic markets | **Diversification**: new products in new markets, the riskiest option |

### Porter's Five Forces

| Force | It is strong when… | Course example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Competitive Rivalry** | Many competitors, slow industry growth, little product differentiation, high exit barriers | Restaurants: low entry barriers, similar offerings, extreme volatility |
| **Bargaining Power of Suppliers** | Concentration and oligopolies, high switching costs, lack of credible substitutes, forward integration | Semiconductor chip suppliers versus automakers |
| **Bargaining Power of Buyers** | Purchasing concentrated in few large buyers, switching is simple, buyers are price-sensitive, they can integrate | Walmart and Target negotiate exceptional bulk discounts |
| **Threat of Substitution** | Comparable functionality, aggressive pricing, higher utility, or latent alternatives (technological disruption) | Netflix and Spotify replaced physical rentals; messaging apps are eroding SMS |
| **Threat of New Entrants** | It is low when supply economics (scale), brand loyalty, capital costs and regulation are high | The automotive sector: capital, loyal brands, safety regulations |

The five forces assess an industry's long-term attractiveness.

### Mintzberg in detail

- **Deliberate vs. emergent strategy**: strategy is not only planned in advance by leaders (deliberate strategy). It also emerges over time from everyday decisions, adaptations and patterns of action (emergent strategy). Realized strategy is usually a blend of both, and sometimes the "intended" strategy never gets realized at all.
- **The 5 Ps of Strategy**: **Plan** (a deliberate course of action), **Ploy** (a specific maneuver to outwit a competitor), **Pattern** (consistency in behavior over time, whether intended or not), **Position** (locating the organization in its environment/market), **Perspective** (an ingrained way of perceiving the world, the organization's culture and mindset).
- **The 3 fallacies of strategic planning**: **predetermination** (you can't perfectly forecast the future), **detachment** (strategists shouldn't be separated from operations), **formalization** (strategy-making requires synthesis and intuition, not just a formal process).
- **The Ten Schools of Thought** (*Strategy Safari*, with Ahlstrand and Lampel): Design, Planning, Positioning, Entrepreneurial, Cognitive, Learning, Power, Cultural, Environmental, Configuration. No single lens captures the whole picture: strategy formation is multidimensional.

### Terminologies and a definition of strategy

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Vision** (strategic intent) | The desired future state, what the organization aspires to become |
| **Mission** | The organization's overarching goal, aligned with the values and expectations of key stakeholders |
| **Goals** | An intention aligned with the mission, often in qualitative terms |
| **Objectives** | More specific and often quantifiable |
| **Resources** | The financial, human, technological and other assets needed to execute a strategy |
| **Results** | The outcomes and performance metrics used to evaluate the strategy |

Strategy involves the **stakeholders** (shareholders, financial institutions, employees, suppliers, customers), the **leaders** (experience, personality, analysis), **resources and skills** (material, human, financial) and **results** (sales, balance sheet, profitability).

The course definition, to be able to rephrase: strategy involves allocating resources for the long term and shaping the organization's scope. It aims to gain a competitive edge by adapting the organization's resources and skills to changing conditions, to meet the needs and expectations of stakeholders.

Four features: long-term performance, the organization's scope of activity, gaining a competitive edge, possession of resources and skills. Four consequences: inherent complexity, uncertainty, a holistic approach, organizational and cultural changes.

## Session 2 – What is Strategy? The rise of Strategic Management

The view of the company shifted from a production machine to a system open to its environment. Each school left its mark on strategy.

### The evolution of organizations: four families of theories

| Family | Authors | View of the company | Impact on strategy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Classical theories** | Taylor, Ford | The company as a "rational" production unit: Taylorism, division of labor, Fordism (automotive) | Efficiency, productivity, process optimization, hierarchical structures |
| **Motivation theories** (1930s) | Elton Mayo, Herzberg, Maslow | The company as a human group: group effect and team spirit, individual needs; the more varied, organized and rewarding the tasks, the greater the motivation and productivity | HR and talent management strategies, leadership, employee engagement |
| **Modern & contingency theories** | Fayol and Weber (School of Administration, efficient and bureaucratic organization); Barnard and Drucker (School of Management, shared consensus, participation); Herbert Simon, March and Cyert (decision-making) | The company as a hierarchical organization that must be administered, and as a decision-making place with **limited rationality**: decisions are satisfactory rather than optimal | Adaptation to specific contexts, environmental scanning, flexible strategies |
| **Open systems theories** | Von Bertalanffy, Churchman, Mélèse | The company as an open system that draws resources and information from its environment (market, customers, suppliers, government) | Environmental adaptation, stakeholder-focused strategies |

Why decisions may not be optimal (Simon): multiple decision-makers, consensus building, goals that are not always rational. Course example: Parliament and referendums.

### A definition of strategic management

It is the function of the company's **general management**. It is not a simple synthesis of the operational functions (marketing, finance, production, human resources). The course metaphor: without general management, the company is a boat crew without a captain, or worse, a crew where everyone thinks they are the captain.

David's definition (2011): "Strategic management can be defined as the art and science of formulating, implementing, and evaluating cross-functional decisions that enable an organization to achieve its objectives." It manages the complexity of ambiguous and non-routine situations at the level of the whole organization.

### A three-stage process

1. **Strategy formulation**: develop a vision and mission, identify external opportunities and threats, determine internal strengths and weaknesses, set long-term objectives, generate alternative strategies, choose the strategies to pursue. These decisions commit the organization for the long term.
2. **Strategy implementation** (the "action stage"): set annual objectives, design policies, motivate employees, allocate resources, create a supportive culture, adapt the structure, prepare budgets, use information systems, link employee compensation to performance.
3. **Strategy evaluation**: examine external and internal factors, measure performance, take corrective actions.

### Strategic management in different contexts

| Context | Specific features |
| --- | --- |
| **SME** | Few markets and products, so determining the scope of the business is less of an issue. The founders' values and expectations persist. SMEs find it difficult to raise equity capital, and this undercapitalisation severely limits strategic options |
| **Multinational company** | Control and structure (relations between the parent company and subsidiaries), allocation of resources between strategic business areas (SBAs), coordination of logistics. Example: DaimlerChrysler and Renault-Nissan choosing between a dedicated plant per vehicle and engine, transmission and chassis plants common to the whole range |
| **Public sector** | Greater impact of ideology and of government control, hence privatisations (telecommunications, steel, energy, transport). Finance limited to the public sphere. Competition is about obtaining resources from the supervisory authorities |
| **Producers of goods or services** | In services, competitive advantage is linked to essentially intangible aspects. Customers give increasing importance to brand image and the associated service: "The future of industry lies in services" |

Long-term objectives are the responsibility of general management. The short-term objectives that implement them are the responsibility of the operational departments.

### The challenges of strategic management

The globalisation of human activities, the development of technoscience, intensifying competition, a concern for global responsibility (CSR), an environment in serious crisis and the new location of the most dynamic economic forces, the rise of entrepreneurship and the collaborative economy.

## Session 3 – The macroenvironment: PESTEL and scenarios

The external diagnosis identifies **opportunities and threats**; the internal diagnosis identifies **strengths and weaknesses**. The main objective of the strategic diagnosis is to determine a competitive advantage that is **identifiable by consumers, sustainable, defensible against competitors and non-substitutable**.

### The environment and its characteristics

De Bruecker's definition (1995): the company's environment is everything outside it (technology, products, customers and competitors, other organisations, the political and economic climate). The company is subject to its constraints without controlling them.

Three criteria to describe it:

- **Turbulence** (changes and instability). Emery and Trist (1965) distinguish four types. Type I: placid and random, no difference between tactics and strategy. Type II: still placid, but survival depends on what the company knows about its environment. Type III: organisations must consider other firms and their reactions. Type IV: complex and turbulent, companies must follow competitors, the economy and the market.
- Desreumaux (1992) matches a type of company to each environment: stable and uncertain, small businesses; stable and structured, large companies; unstable, flexible and decentralized companies; turbulent and highly complex, companies with similar levels of complexity.
- **Complexity**: the number of factors and relationships, and the nature of the transactions (specific knowledge and know-how that are hard to control).
- **Hostility**: competition, availability of resources, social relations, government stability. A hostile environment requires companies to react quickly.

### Macro and micro

The **macro-environment** (the general environment, Thompson 1967) is imposed on all companies and sets the framework of their activity: legal framework, economic situation, sociological context, international framework, technical developments. The **micro-environment** covers the players in direct contact with the company (customers, competitors, suppliers).

### Tool – The PESTEL matrix

| Factor | Course examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Political** | Government stability, tax policy, regulation of foreign trade, social protection |
| **Economic** | Economic cycles, monetary policy, growth in GNP, inflation, unemployment, interest rates, disposable income, exchange rates |
| **Socio-cultural** | Demographics, income distribution, attitude to work and leisure, cultural level, changing lifestyles |
| **Technological** | Public and private spending on R&D, new technological advances, speed of technology transfer, obsolescence rate |
| **Environmental** | Environmental protection laws, waste reprocessing, energy consumption, climate change |
| **Legal** | Monopoly laws, employment law, health legislation, safety standards, intellectual property |

Three method points from the course:

1. PESTEL does not look at one isolated factor but at the **combination of structural trends** in the macro-environment. It is part of strategic intelligence (monitoring the environment and anticipating changes).
2. For each component, note its **impact** on the organisation. Those with the biggest impact on the strategy are the **key drivers for change**.
3. PESTEL is generally used to prepare a **SWOT** analysis (opportunities and threats).

### Building a scenario

Since the future is by definition unpredictable, the scenarii method formulates a few global hypotheses about the key variables of the sector.

1. **Key variables**: what could most affect the business (number of customers, price of oil, life expectancy).
2. **Guesses**: optimistic, probable, pessimistic for each variable.
3. **Scenarios**: the guesses create alternative stories about the future.
4. **Strategies**: a prepared response for each scenario.

Course example, an outdoor party: sunny with lots of guests (optimistic, outdoor games and sunscreen), partly cloudy with an average number of guests (probable, a backup plan for indoor activities), heavy rain with few guests (pessimistic, rent a tent and prepare indoor games).

## Session 4 – Actors and competitors within the industry

An organisation with no competitors does not need a strategy, and the relevance of a strategy is always judged in relative terms. Porter widens competition beyond direct competitors with the five forces, plus a sixth: the State.

### Industries, sectors and markets

- **Industry**: a collection of companies producing very similar products or services (automobile, airlines). It is either a **manufacturing industry** (tangible products, transformed by automation) or a **services industry** (intangible services, growing with service-based economies and technology).
- **Sector**: another word for industry, used especially for public services (the health sector, the education sector).
- **Market**: a group of customers seeking essentially identical products or services, defined by a **geographical market** or by **common needs**. An industry encompasses several specific markets.

### Types of industry structures

| Structure | Number of players |
| --- | --- |
| Monopolistic | One dominant player |
| Oligopolistic | A few |
| Hypercompetitive | Intensely competitive |
| Perfectly competitive | Many equal rivals |

### The 5 + 1 forces model (Porter, 1979, expanded in 1980)

For Porter, the fundamental objective of an organisation is to achieve a competitive advantage, measured by its ability to generate profit (for a company) or to capture the resources it needs (for a public organisation). The tool works for almost any organisation.

| Force | Order | Depends on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rivalry between companies in the sector | 1st | Size and number of competitors, their similarity, high fixed operating costs, high exit barriers, low product differentiation, slow growth |
| Bargaining power of customers and distributors | 2nd | Their concentration, low switching costs, the threat of buyer integration. The powerful buyers are big retailers, not regular consumers |
| Bargaining power of suppliers | 3rd | Their degree of concentration, their degree of differentiation, switching costs |
| Threat of substitutes | 4th | Technology, a difference in value for money. Substitutes don't have to be cheaper, they just need to offer something better, and they come from outside the industry |
| Threat of potential entrants | 5th | Barriers to entry: scale, distribution, retaliation, laws, differentiation |
| **The State** (added after Porter) | +1 | Taxes, laws, subsidies, diplomacy: it reduces or increases organisations' competitive advantage |

### Strategic groups

A **strategic group** is made up of companies in the same business sector that adopt similar strategies. Identifying the groups helps to understand other companies' strategies, identify the key success factors (KSF) of each group, and examine whether changing strategic group would be appropriate.

### Objectives of the external analysis

Identify opportunities and threats, inform strategic decision-making (market entry, product development, resource allocation) and enhance competitiveness.

### Key success factors and market segments

- **KSF** (key success factors): the characteristics of all kinds required by a company in a given environment to achieve its objectives. They differ according to business sectors and segments.
- **Strategic segment**: a group of activities that use the same skills and are characterised by the same combination of KSFs.
- To validate a segmentation, two conditions must hold: **intra-segment homogeneity** (same KSFs within a segment) and **inter-segment heterogeneity** (different KSFs from one segment to another).
- Segmentation models: **Ansoff (1965)**, based on the product-market pair, criticised for giving too much importance to commercial know-how at the expense of technological know-how. **Abell and Hammond (1979)** broaden the scope by taking into account the function performed by the offer.

## Business cases and how to answer a case

A case is solved by applying the course tools in order (external, then industry, then options), and by ending with an argued recommendation. Listing tools without concluding loses marks.

### Free Mobile case (sessions 1 and 2, professor's answer)

1. **Free's business model** differs in every respect. **Value proposition**: two very simple packages (unlimited communication, and a basic offer with 1 hour of calls), with no cell phone charge, no commitment and a very low price. **Value architecture**: direct sales via the internet and telephone, no stores, and outsourced infrastructure. **Profit equation**: low unit sales but high volumes, low operating costs and capital employed.
2. **Why a new entrant?** Mastery of infrastructures was a dogma of the industry, and incumbents were paid per communication minute, so unlimited calls made no sense to them. A new entrant with no established customer base could more easily challenge the rules of the game.
3. **Why did incumbents copy Free?** Because they had no choice: Free very quickly captured 13% of the market.
4. **Has Free created a new market?** Yes in volume: it attracted customers who considered a subscription too expensive. No in value: the price war cut average revenue per subscriber by 33%, and the value of the market with it.

### The three Fally 2025 cases (Apple, Uber, TSMC)

These cases are in the Drive without answers. All three are about a choice between going it alone, partnering or protecting oneself.

| Case | Dilemma | Options in the case | Tools to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Apple 2025** | How far to push vertical integration (chips, the modem bought from Intel, AI, services) | Targeted acquisitions (AI, modem, packaging, cloud), deeper partnerships (TSMC, equipment makers), organic growth (Apple Intelligence, AR/VR SDKs) | 5 forces (bargaining power of suppliers such as TSMC), PESTEL (geopolitics, antitrust regulation), ecosystem and lock-in |
| **Uber 2025** | Tesla's (Cybercab) and Waymo's robotaxis: a threat of substitution for the platform model | Stay a neutral multi-partner platform, deep exclusive partnerships (Lucid, Nuro), vertical integration into autonomous vehicles | Threat of substitutes and of new entrants, core competences (network effects), driver costs (about USD 19 billion a year) |
| **TSMC 2025** | Over 70% of the foundry market but capacity concentrated in Taiwan, a risk area | Geographic diversification (Arizona, Germany, Japan, India), alliances (governments, ASML, fabless customers), strengthening Taiwan | PESTEL (mainly P and L), geopolitical scenarios, 5 + 1 forces (role of the State) |

Key figures from the cases, to be precise:

- Apple: over USD 600 billion of announced investment, an Advanced Manufacturing Fund doubled to USD 10 billion, USD 110 billion of services revenue targeted by end 2025.
- Uber: USD 47 billion of gross bookings in Q1 2025, revenue +18%, USD 300 million invested in Lucid for 20,000 autonomous SUVs from 2026. Robotaxi market: under USD 1 billion in 2023, close to USD 50 billion in 2030.
- TSMC: USD 75 billion of revenue (+18%), 52% gross margin, USD 40 billion of capex in 2025.

### Answer structure for a case (1h30)

1. **Problem** in one sentence: the manager's dilemma.
2. **External diagnosis**: a PESTEL limited to the 3 or 4 key drivers for change, then the 5 + 1 forces on the industry.
3. **Internal diagnosis**: strengths and weaknesses, core competences, business model.
4. **Options**: advantages, risks and costs of each, in a table if possible.
5. **Recommendation**: one option, justified by the diagnosis, with its risks and one condition for success.
6. Quote at least one author: Porter for the forces, Ansoff for growth, Mintzberg if the strategy evolved in an emergent way.
