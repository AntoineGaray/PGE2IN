## Chap 1 – Introduction to Management Accounting

Management accounting produces figures to help managers decide: calculate margins, set costs, find the break-even point and evaluate performance.

### Three types of companies

| Type | What it sells | Inventory | Course examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Service | An intangible service | None | Banks, law firms, insurance companies, advertising agencies |
| Merchandising | Products bought then resold (retailers and wholesalers) | 1 account: Merchandise Inventory | Carrefour, Auchan, Amazon |
| Manufacturing | Products made from raw materials | 3 accounts: Raw materials, Work in process, Finished goods | Danone, Peugeot/Renault, ASUS |

The three inventories of a manufacturer:

- **Raw materials**: materials, with no labor.
- **Work in process (WIP)**: partially completed products, i.e. materials + labor + manufacturing overhead, not yet finished.
- **Finished goods**: completed products, not yet sold, carrying all their production costs.

A **retailer** sells to the final consumer. A **wholesaler** buys in bulk from manufacturers and resells to retailers.

### The manager's three responsibilities

1. **Planning**: setting goals and how to reach them. Examples: opening stores to sell more, reducing opening hours to cut staff costs. The **budget** is the quantitative expression of the plan.
2. **Directing**: running day-to-day operations. Examples: adjusting marketing with weekly sales reports, adjusting material usage with cost reports.
3. **Controlling**: comparing results with the plan and correcting. Examples: budgeted versus actual sales, budgeted versus actual costs.

### Financial accounting and management accounting

The course slides on this point are images, unreadable in the PDF. The table below follows the standard comparison of the Garrison/Seal textbook, the course reference.

| Criterion | Financial accounting | Management accounting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Users | External: shareholders, banks, government | Internal: managers |
| Focus | The past | The future (decisions, budgets) |
| Rules | Mandatory (accounting standards, IFRS/French GAAP) | No imposed standards, usefulness comes first |
| Precision | Exact, verifiable | Relevance and timeliness over precision |
| Level | The company as a whole | Segments: products, customers, departments |
| Obligation | Legal | Optional |

### Role and ethics of the management accountant

Their missions: keep accurate accounts, plan, analyse and interpret data, support decisions. Technology is changing the job. Ethics means doing what is right whatever the consequences. Examples of unethical behaviour given in class: reimbursing false expense reports, manipulating profit, doing work one is not qualified for.

### The value chain

The value chain gathers all the activities that add value, from the product idea to delivery to the customer: R&D, Design, Purchases/Production, Marketing, Distribution, Customer service.

## Chap 2 – Cost terminology

A cost is always classified **with respect to a cost object**: the same cost can be direct for a department and indirect for a product.

### Cost object

Anything for which a manager wants a separate measurement of cost: a product (a BMW X6), a service (the dealer hotline), a project (R&D), a customer (a dealer), an activity (setting up machines), a department (HSE), a model, a sales channel, a geographical area.

### Direct or indirect

- **Direct cost**: related to the cost object and traced to it easily (traced). Examples: a car's steering wheel, parts, assembly-line wages.
- **Indirect cost**: cannot be traced easily to the cost object, so it must be allocated in a rational and systematic way (allocated). Examples: lubricants, electricity, rent, property tax, plant administration costs.
- To get the total cost of a cost object, assign it its direct costs **and** a share of its indirect costs.

### Fixed or variable

| | Variable cost | Fixed cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In total | Changes in proportion to volume | Constant over the period, whatever the volume |
| Per unit | Constant (€5 of materials per unit, whatever the volume) | Falls as volume rises (same amount spread over more units) |

A cost is fixed or variable only with respect to a given activity and a given period.

```latex
\text{Total cost} = \text{Fixed costs} + (\text{Variable cost per unit} \times \text{Units})
```

Course example: €20,000 of fixed costs, €50 of unit variable cost, 100 units. Total = 20,000 + 50 × 100 = **€25,000**.

### Inventoriable product costs and period costs

- **Total costs** (internal use): all the costs of the value chain, from R&D to customer service.
- **Inventoriable product costs** (external use): only the costs of the production or purchasing stage. They stay on the balance sheet, in inventory, until the sale. When sold, they are expensed as **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)**.
- **Period costs**: all other costs (R&D, design, marketing, distribution, customer service, administration). They are expensed in the period in which they are incurred.

Inventoriable purchase cost for a merchandiser = purchase price + costs to get the goods ready for sale + freight-in + import duties.

Inventoriable production cost for a manufacturer:

- **Direct materials (DM)** and **Direct labor (DL)**: direct costs.
- **Manufacturing overhead (MOH)**: all indirect production costs, i.e. indirect materials (glue), indirect labor (plant supervisor) and other plant costs (plant depreciation and insurance).

### Controllable or uncontrollable

- **Controllable**: the manager can influence or change the cost.
- **Uncontrollable**: the manager cannot change it in the short term.

## Chap 3 – Cost behavior and cost techniques

The only difference between absorption costing and variable costing is the treatment of **fixed manufacturing overhead**: it goes into inventory in the first, and is expensed immediately in the second.

### The three cost behaviors

On a graph, the vertical axis shows total cost and the horizontal axis the volume of activity.

| Behavior | Total | Per unit | Graph | Equation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Variable | Proportional to volume | Constant | Line starting at 0, slope = unit variable cost | y = vx |
| Fixed | Constant | Falls as volume rises | Horizontal line | y = F |
| Mixed | Rises with volume | Falls as volume rises | Line starting at F | y = vx + F |

```latex
Y = vX + F
```

Y = total cost, v = variable cost per unit of activity, X = volume, F = fixed cost for the period. Example of a mixed cost: sales staff pay, with a fixed part (base salary) and a variable part (commissions).

To separate v and F from two activity levels, the standard textbook method is the **high-low method**: v = (cost at the highest level − cost at the lowest level) / (highest volume − lowest volume), then F = total cost − v × volume, at either point. It does not appear word for word in the slide text.

### Why calculate full costs

To calculate profit per product, help set selling prices, monitor budgeted costs and variances, and because external reporting requires it.

### Absorption (full) costing and variable costing

| | Absorption (full) costing | Variable costing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product cost | DM + DL + Variable MOH + **Fixed MOH** | DM + DL + Variable MOH |
| Fixed MOH | In inventory, expensed when sold | Period cost, expensed in the period |
| Use | Mandatory for external reporting | Internal decisions, not allowed externally |
| Income statement | Costs classified direct/indirect, subtotal = **Gross margin** | Costs classified variable/fixed, subtotal = **Contribution margin** |

The full cost method (*coût de revient* in French) in three steps: 1) purchase cost of materials, 2) production cost of finished goods, 3) total cost.

Formulas:

- COGS (absorption) = unit product cost × units **sold**.
- Unit product cost (absorption) = (DM + DL + total MOH) / units **produced**, i.e. DM/u + DL/u + MOH/units produced.
- Unit variable product cost = DM/u + DL/u + Variable MOH/u.
- Total variable costs (variable costing) = variable COGS + variable selling and administrative expenses.

### The two income statements (O'Neill's Products example)

Data: 12,000 units sold at €65, variable production cost €35/u, variable operating expenses €2/u, fixed MOH €132,000, fixed operating expenses €85,000.

| Variable costing | € | Absorption costing | € |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Revenues (12,000 × 65) | 780,000 | Revenues | 780,000 |
| − Variable COGS (12,000 × 35) | 420,000 | − COGS (12,000 × 46) | 552,000 |
| − Variable operating exp. (12,000 × 2) | 24,000 | **= Gross profit** | 228,000 |
| **= Contribution margin** | 336,000 | − Operating exp. (24,000 + 85,000) | 109,000 |
| − Fixed MOH | 132,000 | | |
| − Fixed operating exp. | 85,000 | | |
| **= Operating income** | **119,000** | **= Operating income** | **119,000** |

The absorption unit cost is €46 = 35 + 132,000 / 12,000. The two incomes are equal here because production = sales: all the fixed MOH is expensed in both cases. Same thing in the Lee Dress example (income of €40,000 with both methods).

### When the two incomes differ

This rule comes from the textbook, not from the slide text, but it often comes up in exercises.

- **Production > sales** (inventory rises): absorption income > variable income, because part of the fixed MOH stays in inventory.
- **Production < sales** (inventory falls): absorption income < variable income, because the inventory sold brings fixed MOH from past periods.
- Income difference = fixed MOH per unit × change in inventory in units.

### Why variable costing helps decision-making

It separates fixed and variable costs, so it leads straight into CVP analysis and makes it quick to calculate the effect of a change in sales on profit.

## Chap 4 – Contribution margin and break-even point

The whole CVP (Cost-Volume-Profit) model rests on a single equation: **profit = CM − FC**. The break-even point is the volume at which the contribution margin exactly covers fixed costs.

### Course notation

| Abbreviation | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| VC / uVC | Variable costs / unit variable cost (constant whatever the volume) |
| FC | Fixed costs |
| Q | Quantity or level of activity |
| SP | Selling price |
| TO | Turnover = revenue |
| CM / uCM | Contribution margin / unit contribution margin |
| PM | Profit margin = profit |

### Contribution margin

```latex
CM = TO - VC \qquad uCM = SP - uVC \qquad CM = Q \times uCM
```

```latex
PM = TO - VC - FC = CM - FC = TO - TC
```

```latex
CM\ ratio = \frac{CM}{TO} = \frac{uCM}{SP} = 1 - \frac{uVC}{SP}
```

The uCM is what each additional unit sold adds to profit, once fixed costs are covered.

### Break-even point (BEP)

The BEP is the level of activity at which profit is zero. Beyond it, the company makes money. It measures operating risk: how much must be sold before starting to make money.

```latex
Q^* = \frac{FC}{uCM} = \frac{FC}{SP - uVC} \qquad BEP(TO) = Q^* \times SP = \frac{FC}{CM\ ratio}
```

Proof (to be able to redo): PM = 0, so CM − FC = 0, so uCM × Q = FC, so Q = FC / uCM.

**Always round the BEP in units up to the next whole number**: 624.2 passengers gives 625, otherwise you are still making a loss.

On the graph, the line TO = Q × SP starts at 0. The line TC = FC + Q × uVC starts at FC, with a slope equal to uVC. The BEP is where they cross, with losses to the left and profits to the right.

Course example, a Paris–Dublin flight: FC = €50,000, ticket at €100, variable cost of €20 per passenger.

- Q* = 50,000 / (100 − 20) = **625 passengers**.
- CM ratio = 1 − 20/100 = 0.8.
- BEP(TO) = 50,000 / 0.8 = 625 × 100 = **€62,500**.

### Indifference threshold

It compares two options with different cost structures: buy or rent, make or outsource, flat fee or pay-per-use, cheap equipment that is costly to maintain. The threshold is the volume at which the two total costs are equal.

```latex
Q \times uVC_1 + FC_1 = Q \times uVC_2 + FC_2 \;\Rightarrow\; Q = \frac{FC_1 - FC_2}{uVC_2 - uVC_1}
```

- **Below the threshold**: choose the option with the lowest fixed costs.
- **Above the threshold**: choose the option with the lowest unit variable cost.

Course example, renting a scooter: option 1 = €8,000 + €5/km, option 2 = €5,000 + €6.5/km.

- Threshold = (8,000 − 5,000) / (6.5 − 5) = **2,000 km**.
- For 3,500 km a year, we are above the threshold, so option 1 (lowest variable cost) is best. Check: option 1 = 8,000 + 17,500 = €25,500, option 2 = 5,000 + 22,750 = €27,750.

### Other uses of CVP and limits

The chapter outline announces these two parts, but their text cannot be read in the PDF. Here are the standard tools and limits from the Garrison/Seal textbook.

- **Target profit**: Q = (FC + target profit) / uCM.
- **Margin of safety** = actual or expected sales − sales at the BEP, in units or in €. As a percentage: margin of safety / sales.
- **Operating leverage** = CM / operating income. A change of x% in sales changes profit by x% × operating leverage.
- **Limits of the model**: it assumes a constant selling price and unit variable cost (a linear relationship), constant fixed costs within the relevant range, all production sold, and a constant sales mix when there are several products.

## Chap 5 – Full costing (parts I and II)

Full costing assigns **all** costs, direct and indirect, to a cost object to calculate its margin. The professor's central message: the "true cost" does not exist. The result depends on the allocation key chosen.

### Principle and formulas

Objective: calculate a full cost per unit, using the direct/indirect typology, to derive a margin per unit or per cost object. Uses: setting prices, studying profitability, valuing inventory.

```latex
Full\ cost = DC + IC \qquad PM = TO - Full\ cost
```

```latex
PM/u = SP - (DC/u + IC/u) = MDC/u - IC/u
```

MDC = margin on direct costs = TO − DC.

### Cost driver and allocation method

Indirect costs (IC) cannot be traced to products, so they must be allocated with an allocation key, the **cost driver (CD)**. It is a variable correlated with the consumption of IC.

- Prefer **physical** CDs: machine hours, direct labor hours, number of orders.
- Avoid **financial** CDs (revenue, labor cost): they are too far removed from operations.
- A good CD is **correlated** with resource consumption; it is not necessarily its cause.

The method, valid for any CD:

1. Count the CD units consumed by each cost object.
2. Calculate the total number of CD units.
3. Calculate the unit cost of the CD: uCD = IC / total number of CD units.
4. Multiply uCD by the CD units consumed by each cost object. The result is the share of IC it absorbs.

### The three allocation methods

| Method | Principle | Strengths | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Simple allocation (single absorption rate) | All IC pooled, one single CD | Simple and quick, suited to small, little-diversified companies | Hard to find the right CD |
| Organisational cost centres | IC split by centre, one CD per centre, two-step allocation | Many IC become direct for the centres, which are real responsibility centres, so the origin of variances can be traced | Complex if there are many centres. More than half of a main centre's IC may come from auxiliary centres. Centres are not necessarily homogeneous |
| Activity-Based Costing (ABC) | Activities consume resources, products consume activities | More CDs, so more precision, and a cross-departmental view | Heavy to set up, keeps the problems of CD relevance and heterogeneity |

### Simple allocation example: the LU machine

Machine depreciation: €10,000 a year. Production: Granola 7,000 units and 3,000 h, Mikado 1,000 units and 4,000 h, Petit Lu 2,000 units and 3,000 h.

| Product | CD = quantities | CD = machine hours |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Granola | 70% → €7,000 | 30% → €3,000 |
| Mikado | 10% → €1,000 | 40% → €4,000 |
| Petit Lu | 20% → €2,000 | 30% → €3,000 |

With quantities, Granola pays for the machine instead of Mikado. The right CD is **machine time**, because it is linked to actual use of the machine. Careful: the correction slide mentions "2, 6 and 2 months" (€2,000, €6,000 and €2,000), which does not match the hours in the statement. The table above follows the hours in the statement; worth checking with the professor if the case comes up.

| | Cost object 1 | Cost object 2 | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Turnover | TO1 | TO2 | TO |
| − Direct costs | DC1 | DC2 | DC |
| = Margin on DC | MDC1 | MDC2 | MDC |
| − Allocated indirect costs | IC1 | IC2 | Total IC |
| = Profit margin | PM1 | PM2 | PM |

### Homogeneity and cross-subsidization (part II)

Full costing solves two problems at once: calculating a cost per unit (allocation key) and explaining resource consumption (cost driver). IC can only be pooled with a single CD if the pool is **homogeneous**, i.e. if all its items behave like the CD.

Course example (Bouquin), €100 of IC to allocate between A and B:

| Case | A | B | DL / RM | Allocation by DL | Allocation by RM | Conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 3 h DL, 9 kg RM | 1 h DL, 3 kg RM | 3/9 = 1/3 for both | A €75, B €25 | A €75, B €25 | Stable proportions, costs are **homogeneous**: both CDs give the same result |
| 2 | 3 h DL, 3 kg RM | 1 h DL, 9 kg RM | 3/3 versus 1/9 | A €75, B €25 | A €25, B €75 | Different proportions, costs are **heterogeneous**: no key gives the actual cost (€45 and €55) |

**Cross-subsidization** is improper allocation: some products receive too much IC while others receive too little. A cost is a construction: it is the answer to a question, which depends on choices (scope, CD) that are often invisible. The consistency of the calculation must always be questioned.

### Organisational cost centres: the two-step allocation

- **Main centre**: an activity directly linked to products or customers (production workshops, sales department).
- **Auxiliary centre**: an internal service provider for the main centres (head office, maintenance, HR).
- **Step 1 (primary allocation)**: the IC of the auxiliary centres are allocated to the main centres.
- **Step 2 (secondary allocation)**: the IC of the main centres, including what they received from the auxiliaries, are allocated to the products.

Course example: product A sells at €170 with €100 of materials. There is €11.7 million of IC to allocate.

| | Support | Maintenance | Workshop 1 | Workshop 2 | Assembling | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Initial IC | 1,800,000 | 1,500,000 | 3,050,000 | 2,750,000 | 2,600,000 | 11,700,000 |
| Support allocation (DL hours, €0.45/h) | − 1,800,000 | | 450,000 (1 M h) | 450,000 (1 M h) | 900,000 (2 M h) | 0 |
| Maintenance allocation (maint. hours, €100/h) | | − 1,500,000 | 800,000 (8,000 h) | 600,000 (6,000 h) | 100,000 (1,000 h) | 0 |
| Total after primary allocation | 0 | 0 | 4,300,000 | 3,800,000 | 3,600,000 | 11,700,000 |
| Cost driver | | | Machine hours | Machine hours | DL hours | |
| CD volume | | | 2,000,000 h | 1,000,000 h | 2,000,000 h | |
| Unit cost driver | | | €2.15/h | €3.80/h | €1.80/h | |

Full cost of product A:

- Materials (direct): €100.
- Workshop 1: 5 h × 2.15 = €10.75.
- Workshop 2: 10 h × 3.80 = €38.
- Assembling: 10 h × 1.80 = €18.
- Full cost = **€166.75**, selling price €170, so PM/u = **€3.25**. The product is profitable, but only just.

Check to do every time: the total after allocation must equal the initial total (€11.7 million), and each auxiliary centre must end at 0.

### Should a product be dropped?

A very likely decision tree in the exam. Go down one level as long as the answer is no.

1. **Is PM (or PM/u) positive?** Yes: no problem. No: go to 2.
2. **Is MDC (or MDC/u) positive?** Yes: **do not drop it**, the problem comes from the IC allocation. Solutions: question the allocation key, raise the price or volume, cut IC. No: go to 3.
3. **Is CM (or uCM) positive?** Yes: **do not drop it**, the product covers its variable costs and contributes to fixed costs. Solutions: raise the price or volume, cut fixed or variable direct costs. No: **drop** the product.

Logic: dropping a product does not eliminate the indirect or fixed costs allocated to it. They shift onto the other products.

### Activity-Based Costing (ABC)

Starting problem: volume-based CDs bias allocation. Example: €12,000 of logistics costs, P1 = 1,000 units, P2 = 500 units.

- By volume: P1 receives €8,000 (2/3) and P2 €4,000 (1/3).
- In reality: P1 = 1 batch of 12 components, i.e. 12 movements (1/10). P2 = 6 batches of 18 components, i.e. 108 movements (9/10). P2 consumes 90% of logistics.

Principle: activities consume resources, products consume activities. An **activity** is a set of tasks described by a verb and an outcome: purchase materials, move materials, schedule production, set up machines, assemble, inspect quality, visit customers, process orders or complaints.

The four implementation steps:

1. Identify the activities.
2. Assign indirect costs to the activities.
3. Choose the cost driver of each activity.
4. Allocate the cost of the activities to the cost objects.

## Standard exercise method and traps

The professor's answer keys always follow the same order: data, then formula, then calculation, then one sentence of interpretation. Points are mostly lost in the last step and in rounding.

### Type 1 – Total cost and unit cost (15' Pizza, De'Ker)

1. Classify each cost as fixed or variable.
2. Bring everything to the **same period**. Monthly rent × 12. Machine depreciation = price / life in months if working per month (De'Ker: 2 × 1,350 / 36 = €75 per month).
3. Bring everything to the **same unit** (De'Ker: €1 of ingredients for 8 crêpes, i.e. €0.125 per crêpe, + €0.03 of sugar = €0.155).
4. TC = FC + Q × uVC, then unit cost = TC / Q.
5. Interpret: as Q rises, unit cost falls because fixed costs are spread over more units.

Results to check against:

- 15' Pizza: uVC = 5 + 3 + 1.5 = €9.5. FC = 5,000 + 30,000 + 7,200 + 180 + 7,000 = €49,380. For 10,000 pizzas, TC = €144,380 and €14.438 per pizza. For 8,000 pizzas, TC = €125,380 and €15.67 per pizza.
- De'Ker: FC = €875 per month. For 800, 1,000 and 1,200 crêpes, unit cost is €1.25, €1.03 and €0.88.

### Type 2 – High-low (Varibole)

Lowest: 2,000 units for €24,000. Highest: 6,000 units for €60,000.

- v = (60,000 − 24,000) / (6,000 − 2,000) = €9 per unit.
- F = 24,000 − 2,000 × 9 = €6,000.
- For 8,000 units: TC = 6,000 + 72,000 = €78,000, i.e. €9.75 per unit.

### Type 3 – CM, BEP and profit (Nusbaum)

Data: price €1,200, uVC €840, FC €6 million, 25,000 units sold.

1. uCM = €360. TO = €30 million, VC = €21 million, CM = €9 million, PM = €3 million.
2. BEP = 6,000,000 / 360 = 16,666.67, rounded up to **16,667 units**. CM ratio = 30%, so BEP(TO) = **€20 million**.
3. **BEP date** (regular sales) = BEP / annual sales × 12 = 16,666.67 / 25,000 × 12 = **8 months**, i.e. end of August. Interpretation: the company is profitable, but a late BEP makes it vulnerable to a drop in sales.
4. Sales + 20% = 30,000 units: CM = €10.8 million and PM = €4.8 million. Profit rises by 60% for a 20% rise in sales, because of operating leverage.

### Type 4 – Comparing two options (Inkomparable, Pourkoipaplus)

- Calculate uCM, BEP in units and BEP in revenue for each option, then put them in a comparison table.
- Inkomparable: Elite (uCM €13, BEP **5,385** pens, €107,692) versus Classic (uCM €6, BEP **10,334** pens, €113,667). Recommendation: Elite, provided demand for a high-end product exists.
- Pourkoipaplus: uVC = materials + variable production costs (Elegant: 50 + 15 = €65, uCM = €55, BEP = **1,146 hats**). Upper: uVC = €135, uCM = €65, BEP = 108,000 / 65 = 1,661.5, i.e. **1,662 hats**.
- The recommendation must always mention market demand, competition and risk, not just the lowest BEP.

### Type 5 – Full costing with cost centres

1. Build the centres table (auxiliary then main) with the initial IC.
2. Calculate the uCD of each auxiliary centre and allocate it to the main centres.
3. Check that the auxiliary centres are at 0 and that the total has not changed.
4. Calculate the uCD of each main centre.
5. Unit full cost = direct costs + sum of (CD units × uCD) for each centre.
6. PM/u = price − full cost, then apply the "drop or not" decision tree.

### Common traps

- **BEP not rounded up.** 5,384.62 gives 5,385, never 5,384.
- **BEP in revenue**: calculate it with the unrounded BEP (FC / CM ratio). The professor also accepts rounded Q × SP, but say which one you use.
- **Mixed periods**: a monthly rent in an annual calculation.
- **Mixed units**: a cost given for 8 or 10 units that you forget to divide.
- **Raw materials forgotten in the uVC** when the statement gives "raw materials" and "variable production cost" separately.
- **Absorption**: divide fixed MOH by units **produced**, then multiply by units **sold**.
- **Dropping a product**: never drop a product with a negative PM without looking at MDC and CM.
- **Missing interpretation**: every result needs a sentence (profitable or not, risky or not, which decision to take).
