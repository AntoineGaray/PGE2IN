**Contemporary Macroeconomics – Mock final exam – 1h30 – 20 points.** Choose **one** of the two subjects: Subject A is on Chapter 1, Subject B on Chapter 2.

There is no need for a formal introduction or a detailed outline. Write a **chain of well-argued reasoning**: each idea is supported by **examples from the course** (authors, figures, graphs, documents) and by **examples from everyday life or your own experience**. Links between the two chapters ("crossovers") are welcome, and so is a light touch of humour.
@method
**Each block of reasoning:** the idea in one sentence → the mechanism ("because... therefore...") → an example from the course → an everyday or personal example → a limit or a link to the next idea.

**Time:** 5 min to choose, 10 min to list 4 to 6 ideas with their examples, 65 min to write, 10 min for a short conclusion and proofreading (names, dates, figures).

The model answers below are written in full so you can see the expected level. Your own answer can be shorter: what counts is the chain of reasoning, the examples and the links.

@part A | Chapter 1 | Subject A – The mysteries of economic development | 20 | 90
@q A | 20
**"Why do poor countries stay poor when their people own so much?"**

Discuss, using Hernando de Soto's analysis and the other authors and documents studied in class.
@c
**Model answer (chain of reasoning):**

*Opening.* Capitalism is not perfect, but it is the only system that has created so much wealth: Angus Maddison's long-run curves show income per head exploding over the last two centuries. Yet in many developing countries there are only "pockets of wealth in an ocean of poverty". Hernando de Soto, a Peruvian economist born in 1941 and founder of the ILD in Lima (1980), asks why, in *The Mystery of Capital* (2000). His answer is surprising: the poor are not poor because they own nothing.

**1. It is not a question of culture.** The usual explanations blamed people: a lack of entrepreneurial spirit, or the absence of a "Protestant ethic" (Max Weber). The Washington Consensus (stabilise the currency, liberalise, privatise) mostly failed, but instead of questioning the medicine, Western observers blamed the patients. De Soto went into the field, as economists rarely do, and found hundreds of thousands of entrepreneurs in the big cities of the South. *Everyday example:* anyone who has walked through a market in a big city of the South has seen how much trading, repairing and building goes on, without any shop sign or registered company.

**2. The real wall is the cost of legality.** De Soto set up a small textile workshop in Lima with two sewing machines. Registering a one-employee business took **289 days of 6 hours**, and cost **1,230 dollars**, **31 times the monthly minimum wage**. Getting a legal title on a home in Lima required **728 steps**; in Haiti, legalising a plot of land takes **19 years**; in the Philippines, **13 to 25 years**. As a result, **90 %** of small industrial firms in Lima are illegal, and so is **85 %** of urban transport. De Soto's key point: these people do not disrespect the law; it is **the State that does not respect them**. *Light touch:* France has its own version of this nightmare, the "laisser-passer A 38" of *The Twelve Tasks of Asterix* – except that in France it is a joke, while in Lima it is the whole economy.

**3. So their wealth becomes "dead capital".** The poor own houses, land and tools, sometimes worth enormous sums (in Egypt and Haiti), but **without titles** these assets cannot circulate, be sold to a stranger or serve as **collateral**. For de Soto, capital is a surplus reinvested to create more wealth, and it is born from the **property title**, which makes assets fungible: they can be divided, sold, mortgaged. In the United States, **70 % of new businesses** are financed by a bank loan backed by a **mortgage**. *Everyday example:* when a family buys a flat in France, the bank lends because the title is registered with a notary; the flat becomes "living capital". A house nobody ever registered can be lived in, but not borrowed against.

**4. The deeper cause is institutional.** Many legal systems of poor countries inherited **colonial law**, designed to extract wealth and protect the colonists, not to let capital circulate. Rich countries went through the same stage: Colbert's France in the 18th century, and the American West, where federal judges took more than a century to unify property rights. This is the common thread of the course: **"we have the results our institutions allow us to have"**, or in Braudel's words, "a country has the economic situation of its structures".

**5. But legalisation is a necessary, not a sufficient, condition.** De Soto proposes to survey existing (including customary) rights and to build a new legal system step by step. Yet this requires courageous reforming leaders (Myerson's point), a few open-minded lawyers – lawyers being, for de Soto, the most conservative group in these societies – and the support of the population. The goal is an economic and legal democracy: "democracy is not only about voting".

**6. Crossover and stakes: the social contract.** Without access to capital, the poor will fight the system: like Rodrik, de Soto argues that capitalism needs a social contract, and Polanyi warned that the clash between peoples and the market "paves the way for fascism". Document n°6 gives a striking example: **Mohamed Bouazizi**, presented as "unemployed", was an entrepreneur since the age of 12, driven to despair by the expropriation of his small capital; **63 other entrepreneurs** attempted suicide in the following two months. Against Piketty (r > g), de Soto concludes that it is the **absence** of capital, not capital, that causes poverty and violence. *Crossover with Chapter 2:* a secure legal system is also what allows a country to join the new global production networks (the O-ring model) and keep the wealth created.

*Conclusion.* Poor countries are rich in assets but poor in titles: "assets without paper", while the West suffers from "paper without assets". The mystery of capital is a mystery of law.

**Checklist (20 pts):**

- ☐ De Soto presented and his question clearly stated (2)
- ☐ The cultural explanation and the Washington Consensus rejected, with the field approach (2)
- ☐ The cost of legality, with at least two precise figures from the course (3)
- ☐ Dead capital explained: titles, collateral, fungibility, the 70 % mortgage figure (3)
- ☐ The institutional cause: colonial law, historical comparison, primacy of institutions (3)
- ☐ Necessary but not sufficient: leaders, lawyers, population (2)
- ☐ The social contract and document n°6 (Bouazizi, Piketty) (2)
- ☐ At least two everyday or personal examples, well used (2)
- ☐ Ideas linked into a chain, with a crossover or a clear conclusion (1)

**Common mistakes:** reciting figures without saying what they prove; calling de Soto a Marxist (he read Marx but is a liberal); forgetting the everyday examples the professor asked for.

@part B | Chapter 2 | Subject B – The mystery of economic growth | 20 | 90
@q B | 20
**"Are computers really a third industrial revolution?"**

Discuss, using the debate on the "new economy" and the other authors and documents studied in class.
@c
**Model answer (chain of reasoning):**

*Opening.* Everything we have gained in living standards comes from two industrial revolutions: steam, trains and mechanised weaving in England (around 1760-1830), then the combustion engine, electricity, the telephone and running water in Europe and the United States (around 1860-1900). On Maddison's long-run graphs, income per head barely moves for centuries, then takes off. Since the 1990s, economists have asked whether the computer opens a third revolution: a "new economy".

**1. What a real revolution would require.** For economists, a third industrial revolution is not just a sector (ICT, 10 to 15 % of US GDP and 5 to 6 % of French GDP). It means that computers transform **the way all firms produce** and raise **labour productivity**, because productivity and living standards move together. And technology alone is not enough: **the organisation of work** must change too. *Everyday example:* a laptop in class can double the speed of note-taking... or become a video player. A tool only raises productivity if the organisation follows.

**2. Solow's paradox, then its end.** In 1987, Robert Solow wrote: *"You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics."* Computers were everywhere, but productivity did not move, so the computer looked like a sophisticated tool, nothing more. In the early 2000s, US productivity finally rose; growth averaged **4 % a year between 1995 and 2005**, and in 2000 Solow himself said computers had entered the statistics. This is the foundation of the new-economy argument.

**3. Gordon's doubts.** Robert Gordon replies that the rise is an **averaging effect**: productivity only exploded in the ICT sector itself. Computing power faces **diminishing returns** (more MHz, barely more usefulness), and the Internet at work is often used for something else: **30 to 40 %** of employees' online activity is unrelated to work, and in the Olfeo study shown in class, employees spend about **50 minutes a day** on personal browsing. For Gordon, the telegraph did more (3,000 times faster), and **1870-1970 was an exceptional parenthesis**: "the golden age of growth is behind us". *Light touch:* in the course's words, those who believe in the third revolution are waiting for a messiah – "Gordon and Jesus" – and Gordon does not believe the messiah is coming.

**4. Why most economists disagree.** Krugman answers that "indoor plumbing beats iPads" may be true, but that "androids" are arriving: speech recognition, machine translation, self-driving cars. If machines can do human work, growth can come back, even if wages suffer. And Gordon forgets the **uses** of ICT, which irrigate every science: a Covid vaccine in one year would have been impossible without computers.

**5. The real proof: a new organisation of production.** The heart of the new economy is the way work is organised. ICT goods have huge fixed costs, economies of scale and **network effects** (Metcalfe's law), which create giant firms and a rent for the countries that produce them, first of all the United States. Michael Kremer's **O-ring model** (Nobel 2019) shows the new organisation: production is fragmented among networks of subcontractors, and the weakest link can destroy everything, like the joint that doomed a space shuttle. The old pyramid of the Fordist factory (Renault at Boulogne-Billancourt) gives way to networks: the Boeing 787 puzzle, the Falcon 7X designed at a distance on a virtual platform, a Volvo with parts from at least ten countries. This is only possible because huge amounts of data can be sent instantly. *Everyday example:* a group project where one member never sends their part – the whole group's grade collapses. That is an O-ring.

**6. The consequences prove the revolution.** This revolution made globalisation possible, **not the other way round**: by fragmenting supply chains, it opened niches for the countries of the South. It also creates new inequalities **within** groups: two engineers from the same school can earn very different salaries depending on the project they join. And it ends Fordism, where productivity gains were shared through wages, weakening the post-war social contract; Rodrik calls for a new one. *Everyday example:* the lifelong job in one company of our grandparents' generation versus today's careers made of internships, missions and freelance work.

*Conclusion and crossover.* Computers are a third industrial revolution, but one of **organisation** more than of raw computing power. Whether a country benefits depends on its institutions – the common thread with Chapter 1. As the last slide of the course quotes Shakespeare: *"The fault... is not in our stars, but in ourselves."*

**Checklist (20 pts):**

- ☐ The two previous industrial revolutions and what a third would require (technology + organisation of work) (3)
- ☐ Solow's paradox (1987) quoted and its end in the 2000s (3)
- ☐ At least three of Gordon's arguments, with figures (3)
- ☐ The answer: Krugman and/or the uses of ICT (Covid vaccine) (2)
- ☐ The new organisation of production: ICT economics and the O-ring model with a course example (4)
- ☐ Consequences: globalisation and the South, intra-group inequality, end of Fordism and the social contract (2)
- ☐ At least two everyday or personal examples, well used (2)
- ☐ Ideas linked into a chain, with a crossover or a clear conclusion (1)

**Common mistakes:** confusing Solow (the paradox) with Gordon (the sceptic); attributing the O-ring model to someone other than Kremer; saying that globalisation caused the new economy (for the course, it is the reverse).
